# Drug Use and Wartime Trauma in Ukraine: A Conversation with Marina Gribanova

## Setting the Scene

The broadcast opens with the hosts introducing their guest, Marina Gribanova, an anti-drug lecturer well known in certain circles across Ukraine and beyond. The conversation is framed as going beyond a narrow discussion of narcotics: it is really about the psychological state of a society under the strain of prolonged war. People living in a state of constant stress are seeking ways to cope, often turning to substances, psychotropic medications, or quick-fix "solutions" that, in reality, often create new problems rather than resolving the underlying one.

## The Scale of the Problem: Statistics

Before addressing her own background, Marina shares data she had gathered, describing it as "sad but necessary" context.

### Substance Use Among Military Personnel
- A 2024 study by the charity "100% Life," cited by The Guardian (April 2026), surveyed around a thousand service members.
- More than a third reported using amphetamines at least once a month.
- About 20% reported using prescription medications.
- 15% reported using synthetic cathinones (bath salts) and opioids.
- Marina notes she suspects these numbers understate the real scale, but even at face value they are troubling.

### Rehabilitation Needs
She mentions her own involvement running a Narcanon rehabilitation center that was damaged (its third floor and roof burned) and which she helped rebuild by raising roughly $50,000 via Facebook since 2022. A colleague in medicine confirmed that such rehabilitation services are urgently needed specifically for soldiers returning from the front.

### Rising PTSD Diagnoses
- 2021 (pre-full-scale invasion): 3,167 diagnosed patients.
- 2022: 7,000.
- 2023: 12,500.
- No further data was available, but the trend is unmistakably upward.

### Drug-Related Crime
- Total drug-related crimes: 34,000 in 2022, 38,000 in 2023, 47,000 in 2024, and 48,000 in 2025 — a 42% overall increase.
- Crimes specifically tied to drug trafficking/distribution: roughly doubled, from about 10,000 (2022) toward an 83% increase overall — essentially a near-100% rise in distribution-related crime.
- Year-over-year (2024–2025): synthetic psychoactive substances overall up 8%; cocaine and heroin use quadrupled; cannabis up 12%.
- The number of dismantled underground drug labs has decreased — ambiguous whether this reflects fewer labs, less enforcement capacity, or labs being destroyed by military strikes.

### Weapons-for-Drugs Trade
Marina highlights a cross-border smuggling dynamic with Russia: weapons flow one direction, drugs flow the other, constituting a large illicit economy that flourishes whenever a country descends into chaos. She notes this pattern isn't new — drug dealing flourished even during COVID with the rise of "dead drop" (zakladka) delivery systems — but war accelerates it further.

### Children and Online Isolation
With schools operating remotely or underground and parents consumed by work and war-related stress, children are increasingly left unsupervised with unrestricted internet access, making drug delivery systems ("zakladki") readily accessible. Marina describes this as becoming normalized as simply "how things are now."

### Overall Conclusion from the Data
Wartime conditions have led to: a marked rise in detected drug crimes, a sharp increase in distribution-related offenses, and growth in the synthetic drug market — alongside a rising normalization of medicating stress and grief with pharmaceuticals or psychotropic substances, something Marina considers has become culturally "normal" even though it masks rather than solves underlying trauma.

## Marina's Background and Career

### Origins as an Educator
Marina is a trained biology teacher who began her anti-drug lecturing career in 1997, having trained in Moscow before Russia was considered an aggressor state. Her original aspiration was to be a kind of teacher focused purely on nurturing students' character and well-being rather than a narrow subject specialist — a role that didn't formally exist, so she essentially invented it for herself.

As a biology teacher, she was required to deliver a single standard lesson on drug and alcohol prevention. She found that the conventional approach never actually resonated with students — people who sat through typical school lessons remembered nothing, whereas when she discovered a particular lecture methodology (developed by a former drug-addicted American who drew on several books and had dramatically reduced crime in Boston), she saw firsthand how captivated students became — sitting silently for 45 minutes, laughing, fully engaged. This convinced her to quit her job and dedicate herself to bringing this method to Ukraine starting in 1997.

### Building a Reputation
- She initially feared public speaking but built confidence over roughly three years of continuous lecturing.
- She rose to around 47th place on a list of 100 notable people from Kharkiv.
- As she became well known, she began facing backlash: bans, smear campaigns, and institutional resistance — despite doing the work for free and initially nearly reaching physical exhaustion while raising two children.
- Television appearances helped her scale: stations that came to film short clips ended up staying for entire lectures, and producers taught her that milestones (her 100th, 200th lecture) were newsworthy events in themselves.

### Opposition and Attacks
- A newspaper once ran a piece questioning whether she "supposedly" held a biology diploma — her introduction to organized smear tactics.
- In Kharkiv, she has been treated as persona non grata by local education officials since 2005, despite her programs measurably reducing drug use.
- A three-part film called "Number of the Beast" was made attacking her, opening with the "Omen" theme and her photo with blood dripping across the screen, effectively portraying her as something sinister.
- In 1999, a sympathetic official privately warned her — over coffee, insisting she forget his name — that her advocacy had become dangerous, since drug use rates had dropped by a documented 2% thanks to her work, and "interested parties" noticed. He urged her to return to simply being a biology teacher given she had two young children. She did not heed this warning and continued for another 25 years.

### Measuring Effectiveness
Convinced that advocacy needed measurable outcomes, Marina partnered in 2004 with narcologist Igor Vladimirovich Linsky, who was initially skeptical (even appearing in a critical film about her) but later reversed his view after she had him provide statistics from 1997–2005. When she plotted the data, Kharkiv region was the only area in Ukraine where drug use had actually declined while it rose everywhere else — a finding Linsky, a doctor of science in the field, formally confirmed in a written review.

### Scaling the Work
- She recorded her flagship lecture onto a DVD originally just to let reviewers (professors, psychologists, psychiatrists) watch it conveniently instead of attending in person — ultimately collecting 36 written endorsements this way.
- Despite repeated bureaucratic obstruction from the Ministry of Education, she pushed the project through, and over two years of fundraising, the DVD was distributed to nearly every school in Ukraine.
- She has personally delivered the lecture more than 4,000 times — more, she notes, than all 26 Narcanon chapters in Italy combined delivered collectively.
- She trained other lecturers, including social workers in Ukraine and Russia, whose job was specifically to tour schools delivering this lecture; she tracked their statistics for three years to confirm effectiveness.
- She argues that training ordinary classroom teachers to deliver the lecture doesn't scale well, because effectiveness requires frequent repetition and practice (she compares it to a skill like sewing or marksmanship) — dedicated circuit lecturers who visit many schools are more efficient than training every teacher.
- A further project to train police officers in delivering similar material was underway, but she notes everything has become disrupted and uncertain since the war — many schools in Kharkiv now operate underground (she mentions six to eight such schools), and an earlier opportunity to formalize cooperation with the city (she was once summoned to a meeting by Kharkiv mayor Hennadiy Kernes) never came to fruition before his death.

## Language Barrier as a Practical Obstacle

Marina candidly addresses why she can no longer lecture in schools directly: she is not fluent enough in Ukrainian to deliver her rapid-fire, tightly paced lecture style (which depends on unbroken audience attention) in that language, despite writing social media posts in Ukrainian comfortably. The hosts jokingly suggest she could lecture in a mixed Russian-Ukrainian surzhyk dialect for viral effect, but she explains that maintaining total audience engagement — adjusting on the fly to what information the room needs — requires a fluency she hasn't mastered for live delivery, even though she believes she could pick up the language quickly if immersed somewhere exclusively Ukrainian-speaking for a few months.

## Current Recommendations and Ongoing Work

Given she can no longer tour schools in person, Marina's current advice is:
- Widely distribute and screen her existing filmed lecture (freely available on her YouTube channel) to children, since in-person delivery by trained lecturers is not currently scalable under wartime conditions.
- She explicitly advises against conducting these sessions over Zoom for children — she considers this ineffective, since the format requires in-person presence to work.
- Parents attempting to address the issue themselves often fail, both because they are slow to notice warning signs (children conceal drug use effectively) and because fear-based tactics are counterproductive and sometimes catastrophic. She cites a real case in Kharkiv where a fifth-grader, coerced into taking drugs, died by suicide after his father threatened to kill him if he ever learned the child was using — illustrating why threats and scare tactics never work and professional approaches are essential.

## Normalization of Pharmaceutical Coping and Veteran Care

Marina observes that it has become socially normalized for people under stress or grief to simply take medication to cope — a shift she finds concerning. She contrasts this with her own experience of high stress tolerance without relying on substances.

She describes personally helping wean a close friend off two psychotropic medications over two years (2022–2023), contrasting this with the Narcanon program's approach, which she says can manage withdrawal without medication in about seven days. She references a film called "The Invisible Enemy" (with Ukrainian subtitles available), which documents how psychotropic treatment approaches for veterans' PTSD can paradoxically lead to increased suicide rates — a danger she sees as acutely relevant to Ukraine's current situation.

She notes a concrete current effort: before her center's staff left to pursue further training and qualification in Denmark, they managed to guide one soldier through part of their rehabilitation program during his one-month leave — enough time only to complete the detoxification phase — which she describes as a promising but still very limited start given the scale of need.

## Rehabilitation Breakthroughs: A Case Study in Releasing Trauma Through the Body

Marina recounts the story of a soldier who came to her detox program with a history of amphetamine use and classic PTSD symptoms — nightmares and chronic insomnia. During the sauna phase of the detoxification program, he reported physically sensing that **gunpowder residue was leaving his body through sweat**. The staff had suspected this physiological mechanism might occur, and in his case it did: once the substance cleared his system, his insomnia and nightmares stopped immediately, his mood lifted, and he returned to the front. She presents this as evidence that toxins and chemical residues stored in the body can be directly tied to psychological symptoms, and that a purely physical detoxification process can resolve what looks like a purely psychological problem.

### Scaling the Solution
- The rehabilitation center currently needs about nine more trained staff to expand capacity.
- Denmark has offered favorable terms: free training for new staff, and in exchange, those trainees get to go through the program themselves at no cost, so they understand it experientially before administering it.
- Marina's "dream scenario" is to specifically recruit **former military personnel** as future staff, since they would be motivated to help fellow veterans. She has discussed this with contacts (naming "Gena Druzenko" and "Khalid," who went to observe the Narconon program and is reportedly now training there).

## The Emotional Tone Scale as a Framework for Understanding Societal Stress

Marina explains that her signature lecture opens with a model she calls the **"scale of emotional tones,"** originating from the work of American philosopher L. Ron Hubbard, who built a large evaluative table with 42 indicators of human functioning. She argues that most of society's current turmoil can be explained by where people sit on this scale.

### The Scale, From Low to High
Moving upward from the bottom, the tones include (roughly): apathy, grief, fear, anger, **covert hostility** (likened to a "knife in the back," which she says is extremely common right now), antagonism, and boredom. Boredom marks the scale's midpoint — above it, a person becomes capable of genuine **stress tolerance**. Above boredom comes **interest**, which she identifies as the threshold for healthy functioning.

### Chronic vs. Momentary Tone
- A **momentary tone** is a brief emotional dip (crying and then recovering) that resolves quickly and isn't dangerous.
- A **chronic tone** is a sustained, long-term emotional state — and a low chronic tone is what causes real damage to health and functioning.

### Tone and Intelligence
Each tone correlates with a measurable percentage of effective, non-erroneous "analytical" functioning (as opposed to purely reactive, Pavlovian responses):
- Grief: around 5% analytical capacity (a person in grief is essentially living in the past, dominated by loss, with only minimal attention on the present).
- Fear: around 10%.
- Anger: around 15%.
- Boredom: around 35%.
- Conservatism: around 47%.
- Interest: around 70%.

People operating above boredom tend to create more value than harm; people in low tones tend to cause more harm than good, regardless of their intentions, because their attention is trapped in the past rather than engaged with the present.

### Why This Matters for Society
- Narratives that constantly drag collective attention into past grievances or victimhood (she gives a loose historical example of being told "we've been oppressed for 400 years") function as a **technology for lowering a society's tone** — and by extension, its functioning and resilience.
- She cites COVID-19 as a recent real-world example: relentless death-count tickers on every news broadcast functioned to push entire populations into fear.
- A critical danger of low tone: **the lower a person's tone, the more hypnotizable and suggestible they become.** High-tone, high-functioning people are nearly impossible to manipulate this way; low-tone people are highly susceptible to being steered by fear-based messaging (she references manipulative fundraising "marathons" as an example of exploiting this).
- The practical takeaway, in her view, is that the goal should always be to help people rise up this scale — which she says is the implicit purpose of her programs and of her co-host's (Yura's) work.

## On Microdosing and "Miracle" Mushrooms

Asked directly about the trend of microdosing mushrooms (she clarifies the correct term is "ежовик"/lion's mane-type mushroom, not "ежевика"/blackberry) and other substances marketed with miraculous claims — especially to veterans — Marina is unequivocal: **avoid them**.

Her reasoning:
- Any human being, in a high tone, is naturally capable of handling almost anything.
- The moment a person starts relying on any external substance to feel better — alcohol, drugs, mushrooms, or trendy "nootropic" supplements sold on expensive websites — they effectively hand over a piece of their own innate capability and transfer responsibility for their wellbeing onto that substance.
- These substances also degrade cognition over time, leaving users subtly confused and sluggish.
- The underlying mechanism is identical to how any addiction forms: you take something, you feel better, so you take more, and eventually your happiness becomes contingent on continued use. This, she stresses, is functionally indistinguishable from how narcotics addiction begins, regardless of how "natural" or wellness-branded the substance is marketed as.
- As a counterexample, she points to her co-host Yura, who maintains constant high spirits without taking anything — by staying relentlessly busy, juggling many tasks and projects, and constantly completing cycles of work, leaving no idle time to sink into boredom or worse.

## Practical Techniques for Climbing the Tone Scale

For people feeling acutely low or stressed, Marina offers concrete, substance-free techniques attributed to Hubbard's methodology:
1. **The "walking" process**: go outside and walk while deliberately looking at large objects and tall buildings, continuing until you feel noticeably better.
2. **Complete small tasks and cycles of action**: choose any concrete, finishable chore — tidying a cabinet, washing the floor, repapering a wall — and finish it. Completing discrete cycles of action is, she argues, directly tone-raising.
3. **Physical labor**: she describes personally guiding a man off psychotropic medication by keeping him engaged in constant physical work, eventually bringing him to a Narconon facility specifically because it involved renovation work. She draws a parallel to an Israeli veteran-rehabilitation model (which she says is well-funded by the state) built around mandatory daily physical labor — construction, painting, animal care (horses, lizards, birds) — as a structured way to help veterans traumatized by the October 7 attacks and the subsequent Gaza conflict process trauma through sustained physical activity and attention redirection.
4. She also references a traditional, generational coping mechanism: her grandmother's and mother's generations dealt with distress simply by cleaning, washing dishes, or sweeping — ordinary physical activity as an organic antidote to depression, a concept she notes children today increasingly label with the clinical term "depression," a word that, she observes, barely existed in children's vocabulary 15 years ago but has become common since.

## A Cautionary Tale: Prescription Drugs Abroad

Marina describes receiving an increasing number of messages from Ukrainian parents who relocated abroad, recounting a disturbing pattern: a teenage daughter, upset after a breakup, is advised by friends to take "Lyrica" (pregabalin) — which Marina characterizes as functionally close to an opioid-class drug — to feel better. She's alarmed that peer networks abroad casually normalize prescription-drug coping for ordinary emotional pain, and warns that this practice, while not yet widespread in Ukraine, is likely to arrive — compounded by her fear that returning veterans will become targets for experimental pharmaceutical "treatment," given the scale of demand and the profit incentives involved. Her stated mission is to make sure people know to avoid going down this path in the first place.

## Alcohol vs. Other Drugs

Asked whether alcohol is actually Ukraine's biggest drug problem, Marina agrees it is the most widespread, but argues it is less immediately devastating because:
- Alcohol's damage accumulates much more slowly — people often live for decades in what she calls "second-stage alcoholism" (a condition she estimates affects at least 70% of the population, based on observations going back to her university biology studies).
- Other drugs, especially hallucinogens, can cause catastrophic effects immediately and irreversibly. She cites the same recovering patient from earlier: during his Narconon program, he traced his psychosis back to a single instance of smoking cannabis at age 17, which she says triggered a lifelong psychological disturbance from that one use.
- She predicts that, as a direct consequence of the war, Ukraine will increasingly see the arrival of essentially every category of drug, with usage rising across the board — reinforcing her plea for listeners to protect themselves and their children.
- Asked whether video game addiction counts as comparable, she distinguishes behavioral addictions (gaming, gambling) from **chemical dependency**: true narcotic addiction occurs because substances — along with other chemicals and heavy-metal residues from medications — are stored permanently in the body's fatty tissue, which is precisely what detox programs target for removal.

## Personal Testimony: Going Through the Detox Program Herself

Marina reveals she personally completed the Narconon detoxification program — not in a paid facility, but by working it off alongside two drug-addicted participants at a center in Kazakhstan, since she couldn't afford the fee. She describes the physical experience in vivid detail:
- After roughly a week, she began smelling and sweating out alcohol — a residue from her student-era drinking — experiencing something like mild intoxication for about three days as it left her system.
- Residues of other substances she'd been exposed to also surfaced: a poor-quality hand cream she'd once used, and, for a fellow participant who'd worked with cast-iron fencing (treated with toxic black paint), the chemical residues from that work.
- She reports that during these release episodes, participants would experience disturbing mental/perceptual phenomena tied to whatever substance was leaving the body.
- Her conclusion: the program is genuinely beneficial for health, producing noticeably clearer thinking and helping the body shift toward a more effective, self-regulating, self-detoxifying state — regardless of whether the person had an actual addiction or simply accumulated toxins over a lifetime.

## Closing Thoughts: Healthy Dependency vs. Harmful Dependency

Responding to a viewer comment arguing that humans can't live entirely "dependency-free" (noting things like games, sports, and books), Marina agrees with a caveat: dependency on things that make you healthier and happier — oxygen, food, genuine pleasures — is simply normal and fine. The distinction she draws throughout is not dependency itself, but whether what you depend on builds or erodes your underlying capability.

### Closing Notes on Her Work
- She currently delivers her material primarily through recorded lectures and seminars rather than touring schools in person, due to ongoing wartime disruption.
- Invitations to speak now come mainly through personal networks and organizations (she mentions a group called "Planeta," which supports people — including veterans dealing with PTSD — in building greater personal capability) rather than through local authorities, which she notes remain largely unsupportive except for a few "brave" school principals.
- She recently ran a two-day corporate seminar on the tone scale in the city of Smila and says attendees often report feeling positively affected for weeks afterward, likening her effect to "recharging a battery."
- She points viewers to her Facebook page (linked in the broadcast's description) for access to her recorded lectures and around 16 webinars, including a favorite titled "How to Create Miracles."

The conversation closes with mutual thanks between the hosts and Marina, with a note that the broadcast will continue immediately afterward with another guest, Tyshkevich, for a segment on geopolitics titled "Trial of the USA, Russia, and Ukraine: Time to Grow Up."
