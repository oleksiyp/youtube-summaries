# Unjust Cities: Why the World's Richest Metropolises Invest Billions in "Fairness"

## The Paradox of Wealthy Cities Pursuing Equity

Dystopian films like *District 13*, *Elysium*, and *The Hunger Games* share the same premise: a world built for the privileged few, while everyone else is left behind. This seems like pure fiction, but such unjust cities already exist in reality—and most people are likely living in one right now.

Oddly enough, the wealthiest and most successful cities in the world—Vienna, Zurich, Amsterdam, Singapore—are cities run by bankers, insurers, and corporations that know how to count money better than anyone on the planet. Yet these same pragmatic places are obsessed with fairness: they build social housing in prime neighborhoods with business-class architecture, put millionaires on trams, and enforce quotas so that wealthy and poor residents live in the same buildings. This isn't charity—these cities don't do anything that doesn't pay off. So what do they know that most other cities don't?

## How Unfair Cities Are Born

### The Berlin Example

In 1902, Berlin opened one of the world's first subway lines. Through the working-class district of Kreuzberg, trains ran on an elevated viaduct directly above the street, rattling past apartment windows every few minutes—something that still happens today. Just a couple of kilometers away, at the border of wealthy Charlottenburg, the same line dove underground and ran quietly from there on.

Officially, this was explained by Kreuzberg's wide streets being suitable for elevated tracks. In reality, elevated construction was simply cheaper, and engineers would have used it everywhere if they could. But the aristocrats of Charlottenburg had money, connections, and lawyers, and made it clear they didn't want a train roaring past their windows. The working-class residents of Kreuzberg had no such leverage, so authorities decided they would simply "put up with it."

### The Paris Precedent

Half a century earlier, Baron Haussmann rebuilt Paris on similar logic. The cramped, unsanitary medieval center was demolished and replaced with the romantic mansard-roofed buildings admired worldwide today—but only the wealthy could afford to live there. Residents of the demolished neighborhoods were pushed to the outskirts. Officially, this was framed as hygiene and progress, but it also conveniently removed anyone who couldn't afford the new rents.

### Measuring Inequality Today

Modern researchers measure urban injustice primarily through income inequality and housing affordability, with rankings available from organizations like the UN and OECD. European cities generally fare better than those in Africa, Latin America, and North America in terms of income inequality. Housing affordability is more complex, since these rankings typically compare home prices to salaries or mortgage accessibility—though in Europe, renting (including from municipalities) is common, so high prices don't always mean low accessibility.

Europe's least affordable cities for home purchases include Lisbon, Split, Prague, Milan, and—surprisingly—Tirana. Among cities with largely Russian-speaking audiences, Moscow stands out as the most unequal by a wide margin, with Kazan, Nizhny Novgorod, Kaliningrad, and St. Petersburg all surpassing Zurich, Munich, and even London in housing unaffordability.

### Subtler Forms of Inequality

Unfairness isn't always about money. Public toilets first appeared in 19th-century European cities—but only for men. Women's freedom of movement was effectively limited by their bladders, since they could only relieve themselves at home. This problem persists today: in Amsterdam, men still have access to three times as many public toilets as women. No one deliberately designed the city against women; planners simply designed for one scenario (men, beer, Friday night) and assumed everyone else would "manage somehow."

### Modern Russian Housing Patterns

Affordable housing in major Russian cities is typically built far on the outskirts—25-story towers surrounded by parking lots, with a shuttle bus to the nearest school and a 90-minute commute to work. Meanwhile, city centers and desirable neighborhoods remain reserved for the wealthy. A similar pattern appears with new hospitals and university campuses being relocated outside city limits: while cheaper land makes construction easier, doctors, patients, and students become dependent on cars just to access basic services—even as ministries and city halls continue to build their own offices downtown.

In none of these cases does anyone act with explicit malice. Master plans never state "let's make life hard for ordinary people"—the justifications are always framed as being cheaper, safer, or more modern.

## The City Built for Cars

The greatest injustice in cities plays out on the streets, yet it's so normalized that most people don't question it—going down into an underpass, waiting for a bus, standing in traffic. This wasn't always the case.

### The Rise of Car-Centric Design

In the 20th century, the automobile became so dominant that cities redesigned themselves entirely around it: streets were widened, tram lines removed, pedestrians shoved into underpasses. Comfort and convenience became available only to those who could afford a car—and the car itself became a status symbol rather than mere transportation. One owner of a tricked-out SUV admitted he'd happily use bike lanes to escape traffic, but felt he needed to arrive in an expensive car to be taken seriously—showing that even wealthy people can support a more equitable city.

In the worst cases, the more expensive the car, the fewer rules seem to apply to its owner: parking on sidewalks, cutting through shoulders, ignoring turn signals—all without consequence. Meanwhile, ordinary pedestrians, whether an elderly woman with grocery bags or a mother with a stroller, are expected to use inconvenient underpasses without complaint.

### The Bicycle as the Great Equalizer

The bicycle is arguably the most democratic form of transport ever invented—accessible to schoolchildren, retirees, students, and millionaires alike, requiring no license, insurance, fuel, or 15-year loan. Yet cities typically treat bike lanes as recreational additions in parks rather than serious infrastructure, reflecting an assumption that cycling is just a weekend leisure activity. The common excuse of "our climate isn't suitable" doesn't hold up: Oulu, Finland, has harsher winters than most Russian cities, yet residents cycle year-round because bike lanes are plowed just as thoroughly as roads.

### Scooters and Public Transit

Electric scooters are the only mode of transport people seriously propose banning entirely, even though cars kill far more people. Public transit—used by 50 to 80 percent of city residents—typically receives far less funding than road interchanges. For many "successful" people, riding public transit feels like a step backward in social status; historically, London's trams were legally barred from the city center for over fifty years because they were considered transport for the working class, unfit for "respectable" cab-riding citizens.

### The Zurich Standard

Zurich, one of the wealthiest cities in the world, treats it as completely normal for someone in an expensive suit to commute by tram or train—not because gas is scarce, but because it's simply faster, given trams' minute-precise schedules and traffic-light priority. As former Bogotá mayor Enrique Peñalosa put it: "A developed country is not one where the poor have cars. It's one where the rich use public transportation." That's the real test of a just city: not the number of skyscrapers or the quality of the pavement, but whether a well-paid professional can ride the bus without feeling embarrassed.

## When Patience Runs Out

### Paris, 2005

In the fall of 2005, two teenagers died while fleeing police in a poor Parisian suburb, sparking three weeks of rioting across France and a national state of emergency. This was the direct result of a century and a half of policy—dating back to Haussmann—that had pushed anyone without noble blood to the periphery. France has since spent tens of billions of euros renovating these outskirts and connecting them to the city center via new metro lines and bike paths—a far more expensive fix than building an inclusive city from the start would have been.

### New York and Berlin

Inequality doesn't always erupt into riots; sometimes it manifests as protest voting. In New York, decades of rising unaffordability—where even people with steady jobs and average salaries couldn't rent modest apartments—led to the election of Zohran Mamdani, a politician so far left that city elites are reportedly discussing relocating to other states.

Berlin faces similar pressures: much of its housing stock belongs to corporations operating as rental businesses, and local leftist parties are campaigning to nationalize over 200,000 such apartments at half their market value—an idea that a majority of Berliners actually supported in a 2021 referendum.

Radical politicians, whether left or right, don't emerge from nowhere—they gain traction wherever large parts of a city feel the urban environment was never built for them. A just city functions as a vaccine against radicalism of any stripe.

## Solutions That Actually Work

### The Soviet Legacy

The Soviet Union's housing distribution system, despite its flaws (lifelong waiting lists, factory-tied housing), had one major benefit: a cleaning lady and a factory director could live on the same floor, and a worker's son and an academic's son could play soccer together in the same courtyard. This mixing prevented the kind of social tension that builds up in segregated cities, even though other tensions certainly existed.

### Singapore's Enforced Diversity

Singapore, hardly a left-leaning state, requires that 80% of citizens live in government housing, with ethnic quotas ensuring no single group dominates any building or neighborhood. Having witnessed inter-ethnic riots in the past, the government deliberately engineers diversity throughout the city.

### Vienna's Model

In Vienna, 60% of residents live in municipal or subsidized housing, often in prime locations indistinguishable from luxury developments. Social housing isn't stigmatized there—it's actually competitive to obtain. Vienna consistently tops global quality-of-life rankings as a result.

### Barcelona and Amsterdam

Barcelona's famous Cerdà city plan placed fairness at its very core, shaping the structure of its iconic city blocks. Amsterdam—birthplace of capitalism itself—enforces a 40-40-20 rule: 40% social rental housing, 40% for the middle class, and only 20% at free-market rates, ensuring developers cannot build exclusively for the wealthy.

## The Bottom Line

The world's richest cities don't invest billions in public transit, social housing, and quality streets out of pure altruism—they've already lived through revolutions, riots, and burning suburbs, and learned that injustice always costs more in the long run. Savings made today on fairness inevitably get paid back later—through policing, neighborhood decline, endless traffic, and the rise of radical politicians promising to "take everything and redistribute it." A just city isn't a luxury or an ideological indulgence—it's the most cost-effective insurance policy a society can buy.
