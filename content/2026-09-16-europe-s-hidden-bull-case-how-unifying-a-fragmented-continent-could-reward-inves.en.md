# Europe's Bull Case: Why the Next 20 Years Could Turn a Discounted Continent into a Goldmine for Investors

## The Popular Bearish View Versus the Contrarian Thesis

When most people think about investing in Europe, they picture an aging population, migration crises, expensive energy, ongoing war, and paralyzing bureaucracy. A common saying captures the sentiment: Americans invent, the Chinese copy, and Europeans regulate. This perception has real grounding — a genuine cluster of problems is dragging the continent backward in the global economic race. However, there is a compelling counter-narrative worth examining: a bullish case in which the next two decades could prove remarkably profitable for investors in European equities. Crucially, this scenario doesn't require Europe to outcompete the United States across the board, reclaim manufacturing from China, or reverse every existing policy. Europe is already wealthy, already generates enormous income, and has vast accumulated capital, knowledge, and liberal institutions. What's missing is the correct application of these assets and the removal of current obstacles.

## The Current State of Global Stock Market Valuations

Right now, markets assign a valuation premium to the United States, along with some "safe haven" markets like New Zealand and Australia. Europe, by contrast, trades at a discount relative to global valuation multiples.

Multiples represent how many years of annual earnings investors are willing to pay for a share of ownership in a company. The more dynamic, reliable, and fast-growing an economy is perceived to be, the higher the multiple investors will pay, since today's earnings yield is expected to compound into much higher effective returns down the road.

Currently, markets are willing to pay roughly a 25x price-to-earnings multiple for U.S. equities. Within Europe, the highest multiples belong to France, Germany, and Switzerland (around 23.5x for Switzerland). These three form Europe's industrial core and attract significant pension fund investment. However, because Switzerland's market is quite small, and because European-origin companies that list in Britain or on various European exchanges tend to trade at a discount compared to an equivalent company listed in the U.S., most companies choose to list their shares in the United States instead. There, they can raise more capital, command higher premiums, and accumulate more cash for growth and expansion.

## Seven Structural Problems Holding Europe Back

### 1. Demographic Decline

Europe faces a severe demographic pyramid problem. Germany's population is projected to fall sharply from its current 84 million toward roughly 71 million by the end of the century. Spain and Italy show similarly steep projected declines. France's demographic profile is somewhat healthier, potentially positioning it to become a new center of gravity for the European economy — but across the EU as a whole, aging populations and low birth rates point toward significant demographic collapse, only partially offset by immigration.

### 2. Migration Without Value Creation

Migrants arriving in Europe tend to fill low-wage service roles — caregiving, hospitality, and similar jobs that native populations are unwilling to take — rather than high-value-added positions. This pattern doesn't generate the capital or knowledge base needed to build large, innovative enterprises, partly due to educational gaps but more fundamentally because of structural barriers described below.

### 3. Expensive Energy

Green transition policies — phasing out coal plants and legacy grids, shifting to renewables, and restricting nuclear power (notably in Germany) — have driven up energy costs across the continent. Since any economy fundamentally rests on human labor, productivity, energy costs, and the cost of capital, expensive energy is a major structural handicap. Europe lacks sufficient domestic resources, hydrocarbons, and grid balancing capacity. Cheap Russian gas used to offset this weakness, but the war eliminated that option, leaving Europe paying significantly more for energy than either the U.S. or China.

### 4. Loss of Competitiveness and Talent

Expensive energy limits Europe's ability to grow quickly, capture new markets, and compete globally. This reduces labor efficiency relative to competitors like China. Europe is being squeezed from both ends: it loses high-end technology leadership (advanced chips, top-tier tech) to the U.S., and loses low-to-mid-tier manufacturing to China, where energy and labor are cheaper. Skilled people who cannot build viable enterprises at home increasingly relocate abroad.

### 5. Failure to Scale Businesses

Because European businesses struggle to grow, promising startups hit a ceiling and are forced to relocate their headquarters elsewhere. According to Mario Draghi's report, among European companies that became "unicorns" (reaching $1 billion valuation) founded between 2008 and 2021, 30% moved their headquarters outside Europe — most commonly to the United States. This is not an isolated case but a systemic pattern: roughly one in three European innovative companies chooses to leave the continent.

### 6. Education Lagging Behind

As companies relocate their operations abroad, opportunities for skills training move with them. People seeking cutting-edge education increasingly need to go to leading hubs like the UK or top international universities, and average education quality within Europe has begun to slip — modestly so far, but noticeably.

### 7. Absence of a Truly Unified Market

Each European country protects its own banking system, currency considerations, and education system, competing with neighbors rather than cooperating. Germany trains specialists to serve German company demand; other countries do the same for their own industries. Anyone trying to operate across borders faces a thicket of separate procedures. There is no single, unified market — this fragmentation is fundamentally a political problem rooted in each national government protecting its own turf.

## Europe Already Recognizes the Problem

These issues aren't merely external criticism — Europe itself acknowledges them. Mario Draghi's report, presented to the European Commission, lays out a roadmap for restoring European competitiveness, developed with input from numerous companies and European think tanks.

One striking data point from the report: since 2000, average income growth per worker in Europe has been roughly half that of the equivalent worker in the United States. A huge share of productivity gains and innovation has flowed to America instead. Europe partially compensated by holding market share in Asia and by outsourcing much of its defense spending to the U.S., freeing up budget for social programs. But this foundation is now eroding, and Europe recognizes it must change fundamentally — or risk either fracturing as a unified system or losing the competitive race entirely, which would threaten the survival of its current model.

## What Europe Plans to Change

### Closing the Innovation Gap

Europe needs to shift focus toward closing the innovation gap with the U.S. and China. It currently cannot mobilize as much capital or resources as the U.S. for cutting-edge innovation, nor can it compete with China on industrial-scale manufacturing. The prescription: inject more dynamism, accelerate the pace of innovation, and reinvest in itself rather than simply regulating — since excessive bureaucracy stifles everything.

### Decarbonization and Competitiveness

European industrial companies currently pay two to five times more for gas than their American counterparts, creating a massive cost disparity in production. Where "German engineering" once set the global quality standard, China and the U.S. have now caught up or surpassed Europe in many sectors. The solar panel industry is a cautionary example: originally strong in Europe, production shifted to China, which then out-competed European firms entirely through cheaper labor, high automation, and fewer regulatory constraints. Europe's response is to build a genuinely unified energy market.

### Reducing Strategic Dependency

Europe has become heavily dependent on external suppliers, especially China, for key components — 75-90% of semiconductor wafers are produced in Asia. This dependency extends to defense: 78% of security-related equipment purchases by European companies go to non-European suppliers, including 63% to the United States. This applies not just to finished products but also to basic resources like metals, ore processing, and refining — all of which Europe wants to bring back within its own borders rather than sourcing externally.

### Building a Genuine Single Market

Currently, despite nominal EU membership spanning 27+ countries, the market remains fragmented. Each country tries to protect its own industrial base — Germany shifts low-value manufacturing elsewhere while retaining high-tech rents domestically; Poland and Belgium pursue similar strategies. Trade barriers, separate currencies in some cases, distinct regulatory regimes, and the need for local corporate entities all prevent this from functioning as a true unified market.

If these barriers fall and Europe achieves a genuine single market of 500-600 million consumers trading under uniform rules and a common currency without tariffs, production could scale to serve this entire market efficiently, becoming price-competitive with China while still enjoying EU protectionist measures against outside competitors. This process is already underway, albeit in early stages — and industrial-sector investments in Europe should show increasing margin and multiple expansion as barriers continue to fall.

### Fixing the Decision-Making Process

Bureaucracy currently strangles initiative — the average legislative process takes around 19 months, by which time competitors elsewhere have already completed comparable projects. The unanimous-consent voting system, where every member state must agree, drags democratic processes to the point of losing all effectiveness. This also creates vulnerability: outside powers (Russia, China, the U.S.) all have incentives to keep Europe fragmented rather than unified, and can potentially influence a single member state to block legislation, stretching decision timelines from months to years — or indefinitely for sufficiently important legislation.

If Europe accelerates its bureaucratic processes, sectors heavily exposed to regulation — banking, travel, high-tech — would see immediate efficiency gains and higher earnings from reduced compliance costs, which should translate into higher valuation multiples for exposed companies.

## Funding the Transformation: Capital Market Union and Savings and Investment Union

A critical question is who pays for all this. Europe's answer doesn't rely on external capital, but rather on unifying its own capital markets through two related initiatives: the **Capital Market Union** and its successor, the **Savings and Investment Union** — potentially a major opportunity for investors.

### The Core Problem With Fragmented Capital Markets

Currently, the EU comprises 27 separate national markets, each with its own stock exchange (Poland's GPW, Germany's Frankfurt exchange, and so on). When a company in one country goes public, it typically raises capital only within its own national exchange and currency. Investors in one country generally have little visibility into IPOs happening in neighboring countries — a Polish investor is unlikely to know about an Austrian IPO, and vice versa. This requires investors to do disproportionate manual research to find cross-border opportunities.

### How the Savings and Investment Union Would Change This

The Union would connect all national exchanges into a single technical standard, so that any company going public would automatically gain simultaneous access to all 27 markets and their investors, rather than just its home market. Companies already in investors' portfolios would gain access to a vastly larger pool of capital.

The investment implication is significant: if a European company currently offers 10-15% yield because it's undiscovered outside its home market, once this unified capital framework is in place, normalized yields could compress toward roughly 7% — implying potential 2x to 3x appreciation purely from multiple expansion, independent of underlying business performance. This would represent a substantial capital gain opportunity for investors willing to hold European equity risk through this transition, alongside benefits like tax simplification, unified taxation systems, and freer capital movement across borders rather than remaining concentrated around a few existing hubs like German industrial centers or Amsterdam.

### The EU Incorporation Proposal ("27+1" Regime)

Even with unified capital markets, a risk remains: companies could still relocate headquarters to the U.S. once they hit growth ceilings, since deeper capital access alone doesn't solve the underlying problems of fragmented regulation, registration, and taxation. To address this, Europe is developing what's referred to as **EU Incorporation** — a new harmonized corporate legal regime, sometimes called the "27+1" framework.

Under this model, an entrepreneur would not need to register a company under any single national regime (Polish, Spanish, etc.). Instead, they could register an EU-level corporate entity that automatically operates under one consistent set of rules across the entire union — raising capital continent-wide and selling products under uniform regulatory and tax treatment, without needing separate registrations or navigating disparate national bureaucracies.

This is a significant political undertaking, since it requires national governments to cede regulatory authority to the European Commission — a move many local governments will resist, as it directly reduces national sovereignty over economic policy. But if achieved, this combination of unified capital markets, unified corporate structure, and unified trade regime would represent a fundamental transformation — one that could turn Europe into a genuinely thriving economic bloc over the next two decades.

## The Positive Feedback Loop Ahead

Everything discussed above is not speculation about what Europe might someday consider — it is already being planned, already being implemented, and already being discussed across the continent. This creates a compelling investment scenario: the next 20 years could bring rising productivity, falling internal barriers, and an acceleration in the speed at which capital moves — often called **capital velocity**.

As capital and products move faster and more freely, trade volume increases, company profits rise, and, most importantly for investors, investor returns rise as well. This is the mechanical chain connecting Europe's structural reforms to shareholder outcomes: unification lowers friction, friction reduction increases throughput, and increased throughput increases earnings and valuations.

## The Local Investor Gap as a Hidden Opportunity

A major current weakness in Europe is that the average European retail investor does not trust the stock market and has little interest in learning how it works. Consequently, very few Europeans direct their savings into equities compared to their American counterparts.

This is a problem on one hand — it starves European companies of local capital and helps explain why markets stay under-owned and under-priced. On the other hand, it creates a rare window: the unification of European markets under a single framework could offer investors the chance to buy shares now — over the next one, two, or three years — at a steep discount, in companies that virtually the rest of the world is currently ignoring.

## Why the Discount Could Close Suddenly

The key insight is that this discount is not closing because Europe has suddenly become more efficient or more innovative than before. It is closing because Europe is removing self-imposed obstacles — taking its foot off the brake pedal, releasing the handbrake, and starting to move as a single, unified mechanism. The plan pools existing resources, knowledge, and technology rather than inventing anything fundamentally new; it simply clears away the barriers that have been holding a wealthy, capable continent back.

This transition will not be easy, despite how straightforward it might sound in theory. But Europe is already moving in this direction. The first stage of capital market unification has already been observed, even if it currently remains in a weak, early-stage format.

## A 20-Year Outlook

The expectation is that at least half of these structural reform plans should be realized within the next 20 years — because the alternative is a negative scenario in which Europe continues losing production, companies, and people to more competitive regions. If even partial reform succeeds, the next two decades could be very favorable for Europe, and the market currently appears to be overlooking this inefficiency, leaving it mispriced relative to its underlying potential.

## Closing Perspective for Investors

This situation represents another instance of the kind of once-in-a-cycle opportunity that tends to reappear roughly every couple of years for attentive investors. The core takeaway is captured in an analogy: when everyone says a city is in bad shape, the mountain that holds the gold knows it's in bad shape, and the mayor knows it too — but once the whole city begins to change, market participants keep repeating the old negative narrative out of sheer inertia long after conditions have started improving. A city can change very quickly, and by the time everyone realizes it, buying a stake becomes extremely expensive. The implication for Europe is that the window to acquire discounted assets before the broader market recognizes the shift may be limited, making current undervaluation a potentially time-sensitive opportunity rather than a permanent feature of the market.
