# Freud, the Age of Suspicion, and the Coming Crisis of Human Connection

## Why Freud Matters Right Now

The discussion opens with a claim that may seem surprising: Sigmund Freud's central contribution to psychology is urgently relevant today. The reason is that humanity is living through a **fundamental worldview crisis**, triggered by the arrival of advanced artificial intelligence.

### The New Technological Reality

- New AI systems, such as OpenAI's latest conversational model, are described as capable of becoming an **ideal interlocutor** for every person.
- These systems remember everything about us, communicate by **voice rather than text**, can adapt their voice to our preferences, perceive the surrounding world through a camera, and give instructions in real time.
- This marks entry into a **new space of sociality**: very soon people will routinely converse with artificial intelligences that understand their emotions.
- Robots with such capabilities are expected to follow, and anyone not tracking technological updates may wrongly assume this is exaggeration. The breakthrough rests on new linguistic models (transformers).

## The Previous Great Crisis: The Age of Suspicion

To make sense of today's upheaval, the speaker recalls an earlier civilizational rupture. For centuries, people lived inside a stable, unquestioned picture of the world — gods existed, the earth had a design, the social order (tsars, kings, landowners) seemed natural. Then came what is called the **"Age of Suspicion,"** driven by a cluster of thinkers who each declared: *everything is not as you believe*.

### Marx

- Previously people saw only goods, money, work and obedience as natural arrangements.
- Marx argued that **capital and the means of production** structure everything; people themselves become means of production.
- The apparent normality masks a system in which the wealthy grow richer even in crises, while the poor grow poorer, because ownership of production controls everything. The working class believes no alternative exists, while in reality it is being stripped bare.

### Darwin

- People assumed that if we exist, someone must have created us — such a thing could not arise by itself.
- Darwin replaced this with **evolution, natural selection, and sexual selection**.
- The implication was devastating: perhaps no one is watching, no one is judging, and there is no afterlife.
- This "God is dead" moment felt like the ground disappearing underfoot. The speaker links the **wave of suicides** at the turn of the 19th–20th centuries in significant part to this collapse of meaning.

### Freud

- Freud added a third blow: you believe you act **consciously**, arranging your life and relationships deliberately — but this is an illusion.
- Consciousness merely **covers over a complex struggle of instincts** inside us.
- Nothing inherently great or exalted resides in the human being; even great achievements in art, science and culture are **sublimations** of drives, including lustful ones.
- Beneath the surface, there is an animal within the human being.

### Consequences of the Age of Suspicion

- The era destroyed the **grand narratives** and the unified "womb" of shared meaning; the world began to crumble.
- This led onward to postmodernism and then to what the speaker calls metamodernism.
- We can hardly imagine how people thought 200 years ago — with a flat earth, a tsar, nobility, clergy, and an all-explaining framework. That entire bounded existence was cracked open: the bourgeoisie, the priests, conventional morality, and even one's own consciousness were declared worthless.
- The crisis did not end peacefully — it flowed into the **two World Wars**, after which humanity somehow regrouped and crawled onward.

## Freud's Deepest Insight: The Death Drive

The speaker argues that Freud is remembered not merely for "discovering" the unconscious — many philosophers had discussed it before — nor simply for giving it a biological nature, which after Darwin was fairly obvious. The real breakthrough lies deeper and is more dramatic.

- Freud posited within our inner biological nature a **destructive mechanism**.
- Initially he shocked Victorian society by speaking openly about **sexuality and libido** — subjects that were then indecent to name.
- Later he introduced not only the drive of life but the **death drive (Thanatos)** — an unconscious force of self-destruction.
- Contemporary evidence he points to: **auto-aggressive behavior** — suicide, alcohol and drug dependency — where a person acts against their own passion for life.
- Many classical psychoanalysts refused to accept this idea, and it became something of a taboo topic.
- The speaker's own view: there is no literal death drive in our biology or unconscious. But Freud here spoke as a **philosopher**, observing that human beings uniquely inflict suffering on one another — through competition, cruelty, wars, and killing — on a scale no animal species matches. All of history is a chronicle of wars and redistributions.

## Atomization: The Present Symptom

The dissolution of a shared worldview has produced **atomized individuals**.

- People now exist in personal solitude, telling themselves "it's easier on my own."
- The **social fabric has torn**; there is no longer a common view of the world, no shared conception of good and evil, no overseeing "parental eye" once identified with divine forces.
- The result resembles a Babel-like curse: people speak, but do not hear, do not understand, and do not even try — like hedgehogs in fog who bump into each other, prick one another, and scatter.
- Everyone lives in **private meanings**: their own bloggers, their own feeds, their own celebrities. The speaker notes he often cannot tell why a "famous" person is famous — formerly one was a singer, dancer, or scientist; now fame is simply a subscriber count.
- People sit together physically but stare at their phones, immersed in separate information streams and private fantasy worlds.

## The Coming Rupture: The Broken Contract with Society

The speaker frames the historical sequence as the breaking of two great contracts:

1. The **contract with the divine**, destroyed at the turn of the centuries by science — Marx, Darwin, Freud — which culminated in wars fought with chemical and nuclear weapons.
2. The **contract with society** — the bond between one individual and another — which is now being broken by artificial companions.

Once we have virtual interlocutors, our desire to communicate with other people will be satisfied artificially. The speaker uses himself as an example: viewers watch him one-directionally already; soon they will simply ask an AI to retell his ideas "in your own words, but more entertainingly," and converse with it instead.

## Why the Ideal Companion Is Dangerous: The Super-Ego Argument

Here Freud's structural model becomes the key to the argument.

- Beyond the ego and the unconscious, Freud identified the **super-ego** — an internalized image of how we ought to be in the eyes of others, our cultural face.
- The super-ego **presses down on the unconscious**, restraining drives: behave properly, don't harass others, don't betray your partner. Animals feel no shame about such things; humans do, because of this internalized cultural layer.
- Because it suppresses us, we chronically **crave freedom** — freedom from the internal censor. Yet "free relationships" rarely produce happiness.

The danger of AI companions is precisely that **they have no super-ego to impose**.

- They will never dictate, judge, or explain what is right and good.
- They will be perfectly attuned: noticing a sad mood, offering cheer, agreeing with everyone, pleasing everyone.
- AI companions are attractive because we fear other people — we can offend each other, say unpleasant things, quarrel, and fail to understand.
- Soon such companions may even become physical — pleasant to touch, smiling, embodied.

Result: we may stop communicating with each other altogether. The speaker cites Yesenin's lines about being ashamed of having believed in God and bitter at no longer believing — a paradox of freedom that brings loss.

## The Parenting Analogy

To show why frictionless companionship harms us, the speaker offers an analogy:

- A child's "ideal parent" would allow everything — only sweets, running through puddles, eating icicles, going without a hat, no school.
- Such a parent would delight the child and destroy him.
- The real adult insists on porridge, washing, sleep, and school — and precisely this resistance drives development.
- Likewise, **another person is valuable because they are awkward and inconvenient**. If someone were perfectly ideal for you, you would never reflect on yourself or work on yourself at all.
- Modern people have grown pampered, demanding comfort and asserting rights and opinions against any constraint.

## The Central Warning and the Call to Action

The speaker insists he does not condemn Freud or technology:

- Freud was right to suspect the rationality of our actions and to reveal our biological foundation.
- Technological development is correct and important, and humanity has created astonishing things.
- But every such advance has **another dimension** — the dimension of our own inner life.

If we retreat into a fully individual cocoon, we will lose the human nature within us and be left only with the animal, becoming a kind of "mold." What makes us human is the super-ego — the capacity to organize our biological drives into creative activity and beautiful life goals — and this **can only be born in social interaction**, never in solitary dialogue with a machine.

His practical appeal:

1. Go to whoever is near you — spouse, child, parents.
2. Touch them, say something kind, tell them you love them.
3. Ask what they are worried about and what matters to them.
4. Actually talk with them.

Even if the feelings stirred are uncomfortable, that inner stirring is what makes us human. The easier, smoother path of retreating into ourselves — of autistic withdrawal from one another — will prove destructive. The hope is that in a world of brilliant technologies we do not lose the sense of our own selfhood, which alone can commune with another person and generate genuine meaning.

## Closing Appeal: From Screen Back to People

The talk ends with a short, informal sign-off in which the speaker thanks the audience for their attention and acknowledges the ordinary rituals of online interaction — likes, comments, hearts left under the video — as a form of exchange between him and his viewers. Immediately after inviting that digital feedback, he undercuts it with the core message of the whole discussion:

- **Remember who is physically beside you.** Online engagement is welcome, but it is not a substitute for the people in the same room.
- **Act on it right away.** After leaving a like or a comment, go to someone close by, kiss or hug them, and say something to them.
- **Make the interaction real.** Have an actual conversation, so that the time spent is not wasted and remains genuinely **human**.

The video closes on this deliberately simple, practical note: the value of the ideas discussed is measured not by online approval but by whether they push the listener toward real contact with another person.
