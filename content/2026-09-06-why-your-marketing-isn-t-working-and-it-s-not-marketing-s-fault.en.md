# Why Your Marketing Isn't Working (And It's Not Marketing's Fault)

Most marketing spend is wasted—not because the campaigns are bad, but because they're being asked to solve a problem they were never designed to solve. Companies pour money into branding, positioning, and messaging expecting demand generation, when the real lever sits one level above marketing entirely.

## The Standard (Broken) Way of Thinking

When a company needs growth, pressure lands on marketing. Leadership says "get us customers," and marketing reaches for its usual toolkit: branding, positioning, messaging, campaigns. More often than not, this fails—which is part of why the Chief Marketing Officer role has one of the highest turnover rates in any organization. They're handed an impossible task from the start.

These marketing techniques aren't trivial or unimportant. They genuinely matter. But they share one critical limitation: **they are purely presentational**. They only showcase and frame something that already exists. Marketing is the visible tip of the iceberg—the last step after the product, hiring, planning, and operations have already been built. If there's nothing substantial underneath that tip, it collapses back down no matter how polished it looks.

This explains a common corporate pattern: a business feels stuck, growth stalls, and the response is a "refresh"—new website, new branding, new campaigns. Six months and significant money later, results look about the same as before. The team concludes they didn't execute well enough, and the cycle repeats. The underlying belief is always: "if only people understood us better, if only we found the right words." But people usually do understand the message—they just don't care, because the underlying offer isn't compelling enough.

Proof this is true: many hugely profitable companies have famously mediocre marketing—Costco, Craigslist, Ryanair, and (for years) Tesla built massive businesses with little to no conventional advertising. They generate demand through something else entirely.

## The Real Goal: Unique Value, Not Better Messaging

Businesses that are genuinely thriving often treat marketing almost as an afterthought. They aren't straining to convince anyone or agonizing over wording—they simply point at what they offer and people get it. What's brilliant isn't the marketing; it's the company and the product itself.

These businesses deliver **value that customers cannot get anywhere else**—not because they claim it, but because it's an observable fact generated by how the business actually operates.

### The Desert Water Analogy

Imagine selling bottled water to people dying of thirst in the desert. The marketing quality is almost irrelevant—taglines, tone of voice, none of it matters much—because the value is so overwhelming and obvious that presentation becomes secondary. This extreme scenario illustrates the ideal condition every business is implicitly chasing: a form of **monopoly**.

No business will likely monopolize an entire market, but any business can achieve a **monopoly on a specific form of value** within its market—something customers can only get from them. This is "unique value," and it functions like a mini-monopoly, bringing:

- Demand that largely generates itself
- Strong pricing power
- Customers with nowhere else to go
- Marketing that doesn't have to work very hard

The core job of any founder or marketer is to push the business toward this condition—where demand is high and you control the supply. This is far more influential on business performance than any viral campaign or brilliant ad could ever be.

## The Trap: Great Marketing, Nothing Worth Marketing

The worst position a business can be in is having excellent branding, positioning, and marketing execution—pointed at an offering that either nobody wants or that's freely available from twenty competitors. That's money wasted: great marketing for something that isn't fundamentally marketable, like putting fuel in a car with no wheels.

### Case Study: Octopus Energy

Octopus Energy, a UK gas and electricity supplier, illustrates this perfectly. It's one of the most successful British companies of the last two decades, built on remarkably plain marketing. Their edge wasn't clever advertising—it was **customer service** in a category notorious for atrocious support (endless phone menus, impossible-to-reach representatives).

Octopus simply had real people answer the phone and solve problems. Unremarkable as a concept, but genuinely unique within that category. Achieving this required redesigning the entire operation around it—technology, staffing, hiring, and incentives all oriented toward being reachable, in an industry that had spent decades effectively building barriers to keep customers away.

Their marketing was correspondingly basic: ads simply stating "outstanding customer service at fair prices." It worked precisely because it was true and the value was exceptional. Marketing's real job is to **communicate truth compellingly**—not manufacture appeal from nothing.

## Finding Unique Value: The Domain of Strategy

Building an edge into the business itself—before any marketing happens—is the work of genuine business strategy, not vague goals like "grow 20% by 2030." It's the discipline of engineering unique value: figuring out what you can offer that nobody else can.

This isn't a new field; strategic thinking has existed for decades. Yet almost no business consciously practices it, for two main reasons:

1. **It's slow.** Strategy plays out over months and years, not days, so it doesn't satisfy the craving for immediate, tactical wins.
2. **It hasn't been necessary until recently.** Many billion-dollar companies became successful simply by being reactive, copying competitors, and mastering basic execution—without any real strategic differentiation.

That era is ending fast. AI and globalization have collapsed barriers to entry and intensified competition to an extreme degree, forcing businesses to actually think strategically in order to win—reflected in rising search interest in the term "strategy" itself.

## The Strategic Hierarchy: Three Layers

A simple framework for building unique value consists of three layers, with a business viewed as a "value generation machine" whose only job is creating value and exchanging it for money.

### Layer 1: Strategy (the top of the pyramid)

This is the unique value your business exists to deliver. Test it against three honest questions—most companies fail at least one and aren't honest about it:

1. **Do you actually know what unique value you deliver?** Not your product category or mission statement—the specific thing customers get from you and nowhere else. Without a clear answer, every decision below becomes guesswork, which is why so many companies feel busy, directionless, and messy simultaneously.
2. **Can people get this value from someone else?** This is where most businesses fail, often without realizing it. You can be genuinely excellent at something and still have zero uniqueness or leverage—common among agencies, consultancies, and SaaS companies, where the value is clear but 40 competitors offer the same thing. Without a monopoly on that value, the only remaining lever is price, leading straight into a price war.
3. **Does anybody actually want it?** Sometimes a business finds something truly unique that almost nobody needs—unique precisely because there's no real demand for it. Winning requires the intersection of uniqueness *and* substantial demand, as seen in how Apple made computing accessible, Starbucks created a "third space" between work and home, or IKEA made designer interiors affordable.

### Layer 2: Delivery

This is the mechanism—the specific product or operational characteristic that actually produces the unique value. Examples: flatpack design is what enabled IKEA's low prices; the handle on the back of the original iMac is what made it feel friendly and approachable. Businesses need to clearly identify what they are *physically doing differently* that creates differentiated value, rather than relying on marketing claims to imply value that isn't structurally real.

### Layer 3: Brand/Marketing

This is how the value gets communicated so people understand and care about it. IKEA's "the wonderful everyday" is essentially code for "nice design, but cheap." Apple's "Think Different" communicated ease of use and personal empowerment. This layer is purely about creatively expressing value that already exists at the other two levels.

Together, these three boxes—unique value, how you deliver it, how you communicate it—constitute an entire business strategy. Very few companies can fill in all three clearly, let alone show how they connect.

## The Bottom Line

When marketing underperforms, the instinct is to spend more: bigger budgets, better agencies, more content. This is usually throwing good money after bad—like repainting a building whose foundation is failing.

Fixing the strategic layer first changes everything downstream. A new CMO won't actually be more talented than the last one, but with a genuinely marketable business behind them, their work becomes easier, more effective, and cheaper. As a guiding principle: **strategy is an alternative to effort**. Both can theoretically get you to the same destination, but one is a far better use of resources than the other. Before spending another dollar at the bottom of the hierarchy, it pays to examine what's—or isn't—at the top.
