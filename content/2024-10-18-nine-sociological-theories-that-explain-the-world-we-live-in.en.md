# Nine Sociological Theories That Explain Modern Society

## Framing: Seeing the Water We Swim In

The discussion opens with a reference to Herbert Rosendorfer's novel *Letters Back to Ancient China*, in which a man from an ancient culture is transported into late-20th-century Germany and describes his bewilderment in letters to a friend back home. The point of the anecdote is methodological: a person immersed in their own culture finds it almost impossible to see how that culture shapes them. To understand contemporary life, we must deliberately adopt the position of that "ancient visitor" and re-examine everything around us as if for the first time.

### What This Is — And Isn't

- These are **not conspiracy theories** — no hidden knowledge, no reptilians or secret cabals. They come from serious humanities scholarship at the intersection of **philosophy, social psychology, anthropology, and sociology**.
- There is **no "theory of everything."** Contemporary humanistic thought is heterogeneous; the world is too complex for any single concept to explain it. Instead, these theories function as pieces of a puzzle — separate research contexts that, assembled together, allow us to dissect the environment we live in.
- The goal is **not a theoretical retelling** of famous ideas. Each theory is presented briefly, then applied directly to everyday life: how our ordinary experience is being transformed, often imperceptibly, and how bringing that into conscious awareness changes things.

## 1. The Consumer Society

**Key sources:** Jean Baudrillard's *The Consumer Society*, the Frankfurt School (Herbert Marcuse's *One-Dimensional Man*), and Erich Fromm's *To Have or To Be*.

- Beginning in the second half of the 20th century, these thinkers argued that human life has come to **revolve around consumption**. The problem is not shopping itself, but the fact that consumption became the organizing center of existence.
- Marcuse's work highlights the link between consumption and a **race of false needs**, whose steady intensification produced not merely a consumer culture but a culture of **hyper-consumption**.
- The object of consumption has expanded over time: from **goods**, to **services**, and finally — with the rise of the information society — to **content**.
- The speaker cites his own article, "Deconstructing the Selling Text," arguing that the entire digital environment has effectively become **one enormous marketplace**. This is not just about Amazon or similar platforms, but about digital space as such — a field of **endless hunting for human attention**.
- Targeted advertising, nudges toward profitable actions, influencer promotion — this constant selling has become the **background of our experience**, a perceived "new normal."
- Most strikingly, people now locate **all of their needs** — from basic to supposedly spiritual and creative ones — within the sphere of consumption. Whatever you want or imagine, you find yourself already inside consumer logic: recommendation algorithms, designed consumer experience, marketing integrations frame most of life, especially leisure time.
- **Counterpoint:** Wim Wenders' film *Perfect Days*, which portrays a deliberately prosaic, simple life. In a somewhat grotesque artistic form, it shows how **lowering consumption intensity** can restore a person's contact with themselves and with their own life.

## 2. The Society of the Spectacle

**Key source:** Guy Debord, *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967).

- Debord's text is less a rigorous research theory than a **manifesto of left-wing theoretical thought** — but it proved remarkably prophetic.
- When Debord wrote, the world of images was still relatively localized: advertising, television, cinema. He nonetheless correctly identified the emerging trend.
- Today the internet is saturated with **personal performances**: bloggers, influencers, and ordinary people demonstrating their lives — sometimes for explicitly commercial purposes (monetizing a personal brand, selling goods or services), sometimes simply as an unremarkable part of social life (posting travel photos, showing where one is).
- Platforms like Instagram and TikTok are structurally designed so that users **participate in producing** what Debord called, in his first thesis, an "immense accumulation of spectacles."
- The crucial insight: **the spectacle is the production of the unreal**. The speaker develops this in his own phrase, "the consumption of illusions."
- This unreality is not only filters, Photoshop, and special effects. It is a whole imaginary world of **false horizons** in which people either **escape** the sad prose of everyday life, or invest attention and time hoping to improve their circumstances.
- Result: we are all simultaneously **spectators and producers** of the spectacle. Pranks, stand-up performances, staged comedy in both the literal and figurative sense — an enormous share of contemporary content is woven from this material.

## 3. The Risk Society

**Key source:** Ulrich Beck's concept of the risk society.

- Modern society is defined less by the production of material goods and values than by the **production of risks**: climate threats, the danger of a third world war, recurring economic crises.
- Risk saturates individual life too. Changing careers, relocating, taking on a mortgage or a loan, retraining, monetizing a hobby — the abundance of "opportunities" offered by what the speaker calls **"the capitalism of opportunities"** simultaneously makes each person a permanent decision-maker under uncertainty.
- Every person effectively becomes a **crisis manager of their own life** — a "risking human being" who constantly calculates how to allocate time, attention, and any available assets: where to study, whether a profession will pay off, whether to invest in crypto or other instruments.
- Even **daily investments of attention**, conscious or not, function as risk allocation between more and less "useful" things.
- This applies regardless of ambition. Even people with no desire for "successful success" live under **high uncertainty**, so raised tolerance for risk and an **investor's mindset** have become the new normal — not just something urged on us by info-business hustlers preaching about leaving the comfort zone.

## 4. The Society of Singularities

**Key sources:** Andreas Reckwitz, *The Society of Singularities*, alongside Zygmunt Bauman's ideas about individualization.

- These theories describe the **twilight of society** in the traditional sense: strong social ties and large communities have become vestigial features within a new social type.
- Defining traits: **high individualism**, self-preoccupation, sometimes described as a **narcissistic fixation** on the self.
- Consequences include widespread **loneliness**, constant switching between short-term connections, and the absence of stable relationships.
- "Singularity" means *uniqueness/singleness*: the person perceives themselves as a **single, individual business project**, sets ambitious personal goals, and learns to treat **personal self-realization** as not merely normal but as the highest imperative — "focus on yourself and everything will work out."
- This individualism has transformed contemporary life as profoundly as consumption, spectacle, and risk. It functions simultaneously as a **norm, a burden, and a tragedy** — visible in the high levels of loneliness among people who find it hard to build relationships under current cultural and economic conditions.

## 5. The Burnout Society

**Key source:** Byung-Chul Han, *The Burnout Society* (published in Russian translation under titles referencing both "fatigue" and "burnout").

- Burnout is spreading widely, and it is **insufficient** to explain it merely as a work–rest imbalance — the classic picture of an over-exploited or over-enthusiastic worker.
- An illustrative testimony: a woman who went to a psychologist half a year after burning out, argued against the advice to learn to rest, insisted nothing brought her joy and that she needed not weekends but "IV drips with placenta and celery," and long believed that a good employee must work without weekends or vacation — "as if I were not a person but a washing machine."
- The speaker's view: burnout is a **global, existential situation** connected to all the other aspects discussed. Assembled together, the features of contemporary society all function, to different degrees, as sources of this **global existential exhaustion**.
- The mechanism: living permanently at one's limit, driven by **excessive demands on the self-realization project** and the pursuit of "the best version of yourself," which corrodes a person from within until they collapse inward.
- Late capitalism thus produces **burnt-out subjects** — people who want nothing and suffer mental disorders that are partly consequences of intense social pressure in a **hyper-competitive society**, where one must constantly fight for the right to move toward one's supposedly happy life.

## 6. The Society of Fashion

**Key source:** Gilles Lipovetsky's analysis of the "empire of fashion."

- This is not simply about people who like clothes. Fashion is one component: cycles have accelerated from seasonal to **ultra-fast fashion**, with collections turning over in as little as a week — a phenomenon linked to the broader theme of acceleration.
- The deeper claim: the **system of fashion has replaced the grand narratives** and big ideas of the past. What now organizes life is the **aesthetic pleasure of novelty**.
- Novelty may take any form — a new tourist destination, a new garment or accessory, a new phone model. What matters is the refreshing of impressions.
- Life becomes a movement **from one impression to the next**, from one fashionable current to another, creating a distinctive rhythm and filling existence with new experiences: new TV series, awaited new films, new places, new locations.
- Novelty of experience becomes an **idée fixe**, substituting for large collective projects — such as the Soviet project of building a grand future.
- Combined with the risk society, this produces a paradoxical figure: a person who is at once a **risk manager anxiously designing their future** and someone continually investing attention and time into fresh novelty.

## 7. The Society of Acceleration

- Much here seems self-evident, yet the **humanistic conceptualization** matters: the pervasive sense that time is short and one must hurry.
- Residents of large cities live in permanent rush — constant deadlines, new projects, the drive to keep up and get into the trend — a model of life defined by speed.

### Speed as a Model of Everyday Life

- The logic of financial markets — where speed of transaction has long been decisive — has migrated into ordinary life. The stock exchange is no longer the only arena where velocity rules; **the same tempo now structures personal existence**.
- The condition is not merely that people are always rushing. It is that they must **fit an overwhelming abundance of possibilities into finite time**: closing tasks, meeting deadlines, absorbing technological innovations, inhabiting multiple contexts at once — and even "managing to have fun."
- The dominant subjective experience is a **chronic sense of time scarcity**: perpetual busyness, agitation, and the compulsion to keep moving forward.
- A striking illustration: commercially sold "walking accelerator" devices that attach to ordinary shoes and let a person walk several times faster (up to roughly 24 km/h). The gadget literalizes the cultural imperative — process information faster, cover distances faster, **keep up, keep up, keep up**.
- There is a telling ambiguity in the very word **"success"**: it carries the sense of *managing to arrive in time*, of winning time back — while the feeling of not having enough time never disappears.

### The Paradox of Acceleration: Things That Slow Down

- Drawing on **Judy Wajcman**, a sociologist of science and technology, the point is made that acceleration in some domains produces **deceleration in others**.
- Examples: hours lost in traffic jams; bureaucratic red tape where obtaining documents can drag on for months; countless rigid institutional processes that refuse to speed up.
- The result is a contradictory experience: a transaction or message can be completed instantly, yet a person can simultaneously be **stuck motionless** for hours with no practical way out.

## 8. The Information Society

### The Cult of Information and Content

- The contemporary order is often labelled the **information society**: a cult of information, of innovative ideas, and above all of **content creation**.
- A representative popular claim (from a video shown in the discussion) argues that factories no longer need so many workers, that progress has moved elsewhere, and that the supreme value today is information and content — so that **people who can process information and convert it into content are "worth their weight in gold."**

### The Real Problem: Excess, Not Scarcity

- The genuine difficulty has inverted. Following the idea of a **"crisis of narration"**, the problem of modern life is not lack of information but its **overabundance**.
- We have access to virtually any information we need, yet we **cannot metabolize** the daily volume delivered by news portals, podcasts, new series, music selections, and endless content formats arriving at furious speed.
- This surplus becomes a source of **intoxication**: it is hard to orient oneself, hard to determine which information one actually needs, and hard to assemble the flood into a coherent picture of the world from which personal meanings and values could be derived.
- The challenge extends beyond doomscrolling and immersion in negative news. It concerns the deeper capacity to **generate personal meaning**, since an excess of information does not equal understanding of that information or knowing how to handle it.

### Postmodern Fragmentation

- The proliferation of **scattered, disconnected sources** is a defining feature of postmodern society: there is **no single semantic center**, no sufficiently authoritative narrative — let alone "truth" — to help people orient themselves.
- Each person therefore navigates the information flow **by touch, at their own risk**, trying to identify what personally suits them and what can help shape a worldview, a life stance, a personal path.
- The benefits are real: instant access replaces trips to the library; any text or video on any subject is available, now with AI tools such as ChatGPT to help package and summarize material. But **none of this automatically produces a systematic perception of reality**.
- Information has indeed become a **new currency**, inseparable from the parallel currency of **attention** paid to it.

## 9. The Technological Society

### Why "Post-Industrial" Doesn't Mean "Post-Technological"

- A common assumption holds that industrial society gives way to an informational, post-industrial one, in which information, services, content, and even lifestyles push **technology into the background**.
- This assumption is mistaken. Referring to **Bernard Stiegler's** work, the argument is that technology remains constitutive: we are **permanently in interaction with technical objects**, often more intensively than with other human beings.
- Screen time and smartphone interaction consume an enormous share of daily life; the consumption of any content is itself mediated technologically.

### Technology as the Producer of the Consumer Subject

- Viewed as **technologies of consumer experience**, technical systems are embedded in life precisely because they help **manufacture the consuming human being** — the participant in the spectacle who continually engages with digital environments and technological instruments.
- Invoking **Lucy Suchman's** work in the sociology of science and technology, this amounts to a **reconfiguration of human–machine relations**: society creates conditions in which a person responds to a dense field of technical stimuli, and interaction with technical objects becomes plain, everyday reality.

### Ambivalence and New Realities

- There is genuine benefit: electricity, the internet, and gadgets that solve practical problems all improve daily life. But instrumentalism also produces **mutations of everyday experience**.
- The case of **artificial intelligence** raises the sharpest questions — for instance, a person creating an entirely artificial virtual "girlfriend" persona and earning money from it. Passing judgment is difficult, but it signals a new reality in which much that once counted as a **human product becomes a product of the technologies themselves**.
- The concern is **not paranoia about a machine uprising**. It is the recognition that the human being is becoming **part of a technical reality**, and that this reshapes life and creates new conditions of existence without which neither our present nor our future can be imagined.

## Synthesis: Weaving the Contexts Together

- All the aspects discussed — **risk, consumption, spectacle, singularity, burnout, fashion, acceleration and deceleration, information, technology** — should be read as a **single interwoven context** rather than separate explanations.
- These contexts intertwine in **intricate patterns**, affecting each person's life to different degrees, and together they compose the complexly organized social environment in which we meet our daily challenges, tasks, and anxieties.

## Closing: The Therapeutic Value of Theory

- The central conviction: **humanistic theoretical reflection**, situated at the junction of theory and lived practice, allows us to make sense of what is happening.
- Its function is to move a person out of a state of being **captured by anxiety** and by an oppressive sense of uncertainty and disorientation — the reflex of "the world has gone mad."
- Immersion in the theoretical context enables a shift toward a **calmer, more sober, investigative position** — and in this sense produces a genuinely **therapeutic effect**.
