# Why Elon Musk Bought Twitter: A Critique of "Woke" Ideology as Neo-Marxism

## The Opening Puzzle: Musk's $44 Billion "Mistake"

The video opens by asking whether Elon Musk acted irrationally in buying Twitter for **$44 billion**. On the surface, the deal looks catastrophic:

- The company reportedly lost roughly **two-thirds of its value**
- It lost most of its advertisers and most of its staff
- Losses were said to reach up to **$4 million per day**, pushing the platform toward the brink of bankruptcy

Musk's own explanation on the day of the deal was that he bought it "to save humanity," which the speaker interprets not as a financial calculation but as an **ideological and deeply personal act**.

### The Personal Motivation

The speaker frames Musk's purchase as rooted in a painful family story: Musk's child Xavier transitioned and took the name Jenna Wilson, while also adopting radical left-wing politics — described by Musk himself as having moved "beyond socialism into full communism," believing that all rich people are evil. From the speaker's point of view, Musk effectively "lost his child" to an ideology, and concluded that **whoever controls the channels of propaganda controls public opinion**.

### Musk's Three Actions After the Purchase

1. **Fired most of the staff** responsible for censorship and enforcing the prevailing ideological agenda
2. **Unbanned prominent figures**, including Kanye West and Donald Trump
3. **Restored the ability of ideological opponents to speak** on the platform

## Two Case Studies: When Ideology Meets the Market

### Bud Light and Dylan Mulvaney

- Bud Light is a beer brand traditionally coded as a **masculine, working-class American product**, frequently referenced in country music, with enormous sales volume.
- The brand's marketing team partnered with **Dylan Mulvaney**, a trans influencer with roughly 8 million TikTok followers and 2 million on Instagram, to celebrate the one-year (365-day) anniversary of her transition — including printing her image on commemorative cans.
- The consumer backlash was severe and theatrical: customers filmed themselves **shooting cans with automatic weapons**, throwing them in the trash, and musicians removed references to the brand from their song lyrics.
- The financial damage was dramatic — shelves left stocked with unsold product, and roughly **$5 billion in lost market capitalization** in the following months.
- Founders and executives publicly distanced themselves and apologized, essentially claiming they hadn't known about the campaign, in an attempt to win customers back. The speaker's summary: marketers destroyed in one week what took twenty years to build.

### Victoria's Secret

- The brand built its identity around **highly sexualized "Angels"** — tall, glamorous models. Men bought the lingerie with that image in mind; women bought it aspirationally, comparable to buying sneakers endorsed by Michael Jordan not because you're an athlete but because you want to identify with one.
- In **2018** the company changed course, featuring models representing diversity categories — disabled people, transgender models, plus-size and body-positive models — in place of the old formula.
- The result mirrored Bud Light: **sales declining roughly 5% per quarter** as customers walked away.
- The speaker's analogy: it's like walking into a nightclub or strip bar and finding a church inside — you simply turn around and leave.

### The Central Question

If these campaigns were so obviously commercially self-destructive, why did major corporations pursue them? The speaker's answer: **they were not free to refuse.** They were pressured into acting against their own commercial interest.

## The Theoretical Foundations

### Power Struggle Through Ideas

Among humans, the struggle for power is waged through **ideas**. Whichever idea is accepted by the largest number of individuals tends to win. Commodity–money relations versus barter, rule of law, democracy, socialism, communism — all are ideas around which people organize, and the larger coalition can consume the smaller one and its dissenters.

### Classical Marxism

A century ago, Marxism asserted a conflict between social forces — capitalists owning the means of production versus everyone else — over **limited resources**. It assumed a **zero-sum game**: the pie cannot grow, so the only option is to take from the "bad guys."

The speaker argues this is empirically false: production levels have grown enormously over the past century, proving the pie *does* grow and that cooperative creation is possible. But if your actual goal is **power rather than creation**, you must insist the game is zero-sum, because that justifies expropriation.

### From Modernism to Postmodernism

- A century ago the intellectual framework was **modernism**; it has since shifted to **postmodernism**.
- Postmodernism holds that **objective reality does not exist**, or that there are many competing realities.
- Consequently, "objective reality" becomes merely whichever interpretation a dominant group imposes — a question of **whose reality is stronger**.

### Neo-Marxism: The Transformation

- **Old Marxism**: the conflict is primarily **economic** — means of production, rich versus poor.
- **New Marxism**: the conflict permeates **all surrounding institutions** — family, religion, morality, norms of behavior, and even **science and the scientific method**.
