# A Guide to Common Cognitive Biases and Thinking Errors

This overview walks through a wide catalog of cognitive biases — systematic errors in how we perceive, judge, and remember — with plain-language definitions and everyday examples for each.

## Biases in Judging People and Behavior

### Fundamental Attribution Error
The tendency to explain other people's behavior by their **personality and character** rather than by external circumstances.
- Someone snaps at you on the street, and you conclude they are rude and mean — instead of considering they may have had a terrible day.
- A colleague arrives late and you label them irresponsible, ignoring possible transport problems or family difficulties.

### Self-Serving Bias
People attribute their **successes to their own qualities and effort**, but their **failures to external circumstances**.
- A high exam grade proves you're smart and well prepared; a low grade is blamed on unfair questions or the teacher's poor explanations.

### Just-World Phenomenon
The belief that the world is fundamentally fair and everyone gets what they deserve.
- Assuming poor people are poor because they are lazy, and rich people are rich purely through merit — while ignoring systemic or random factors.

### Observer-Expectancy Effect
The observer's expectations influence what they actually perceive or find.
- A teacher who expects a student to perform badly unconsciously notices that student's mistakes more and their successes less.
- A doctor convinced of a particular diagnosis may interpret symptoms to fit it, even if they point elsewhere.

### Primacy / First-Impression Effect
The first impression exerts a strong and lasting influence on perception, even when later information contradicts it.
- A student who seemed lazy on day one is still seen as "problematic" even after genuinely improving.
- A satisfying first purchase makes you trust a brand long-term, despite later disappointments.

## Biases in Self-Assessment

### Overconfidence Effect
Believing you know more, or are more certainly right, than is justified.
- A driver convinced of excellent skills and immunity from accidents, despite ignoring traffic rules.
- An investor who trusts their stock forecasts so much they put all their money into one company.

### Dunning–Kruger Effect
People with **low knowledge or skill** overestimate their ability, because they lack the knowledge needed to recognize their own incompetence.
- Reading one article on climate change and considering yourself an expert.
- A beginner athlete thinking they're ready to compete.

### Illusory Superiority (Better-Than-Average Effect)
Most people believe they are smarter, more talented, or generally better than others.
- Students routinely assume they are brighter or perform better than the majority of their classmates.

### Illusion of Explanatory Depth
Believing you understand something far more deeply than you actually do.
- Thinking you understand how a car engine works after a few videos and articles, yet being unable to explain the details or repair it.

### Illusion of Control
Believing you can control or influence events that are actually beyond your control.
- Assuming that being extra careful will let you avoid every possible accident, even though some events can't be predicted or prevented.

### End-of-History Illusion
Assuming your current personality, tastes, and views will stay the same forever.
- A teenager convinced their present hobbies will last a lifetime.
- Assuming your current circle of friends will remain unchanged, though people's environments shift as their lives do.

## Biases in Processing Information

### Confirmation Bias
Seeking, interpreting, and remembering information so that it supports existing beliefs, while ignoring contradictory evidence.
- Believing a diet works, so noticing only positive reviews.
- Believing you're unlucky, so registering only failures and overlooking moments of good fortune.

### Selective Information Search
A closely related tendency to look only for material that supports your views and to reject opposing evidence.
- Only noticing praise for your favorite sports team and ignoring criticism.
- A conspiracy believer consuming only confirming videos and dismissing debunkings.

### Overgeneralization
Drawing sweeping conclusions from one or a few cases, often producing false and negative beliefs.
- Failing one exam and thinking "I always fail exams."
- Being betrayed by one friend and concluding "nobody can be trusted."

### Amplification
Exaggerating certain aspects of information or events, positively or negatively — essentially making a mountain out of a molehill.
- A minor mistake at work convinces you that you'll certainly be fired.
- A polite compliment convinces you that you're the best in the field.

### Contrast Effect
Perception of something changes depending on what it's compared with.
- After holding a hot object, a warm one feels cold.
- Showing an expensive product first makes the cheaper one seem like an even better bargain.

### Frequency Illusion (Baader–Meinhof Phenomenon)
After first learning of something, you start noticing it everywhere, though its actual frequency hasn't changed.
- A newly learned word suddenly appears in conversations, articles, and on TV.
- A newly discovered car model seems to be on every street.

### Proportionality Bias
Assuming that large or unusual events must have equally large or unusual causes.
- Attributing a startup's success entirely to the founder's genius or a unique idea, when luck, timing, and solid management may have mattered more.

### Outcome Bias
Judging the quality of a decision by its result rather than by the reasoning process behind it.
- Putting all your money into one stock is called "a brilliant decision" if it soars — and "a terrible decision" if it crashes, even though the decision itself was identical.

## Biases in Decision-Making and Valuation

### Endowment Effect
People value things more highly simply because they own them.
- Believing your house is better than the identical one next door because you're attached to it and invested effort in it.
- Overpricing an old phone or piece of furniture online because it feels more valuable to you.

### Zero-Risk Preference
Choosing options that eliminate risk entirely, even when they aren't the most beneficial.
- Skipping an interesting trip out of fear of flying, despite the tiny probability of a crash.
- Keeping savings under the mattress instead of investing in stocks or bonds that could yield far more.

### Status Quo Bias
Preferring things to stay as they are and avoiding change, even when change would bring better outcomes.
- Staying in a disliked job despite better offers, simply because it's familiar.
- Ordering the same dish every time, even in a new restaurant with a varied menu.

### Escalation of Commitment (Irrational Escalation)
Continuing to invest time, money, and effort into a failing venture because of what has already been invested, and to avoid admitting a mistake.
- Finishing a boring film because you've already spent an hour on it.
- Staying in a bad relationship so the years already spent don't feel wasted.

### Less-Is-Better Effect
Preferring a smaller option that appears fuller or more generous when items are judged separately.
- Choosing ice cream in a small cup filled to the brim over the same amount in a large cup, because it seems like more.

## Social and Authority-Driven Biases

### Authority Bias
Trusting the opinions or instructions of a perceived expert, even when they may be wrong.
- Following a doctor's medication advice without question because they're the expert.
- Buying a product because a celebrity or well-known specialist endorses it.

### Conformity
Changing behavior, opinions, or beliefs to match group expectations — the drive to fit in and not stand out.
- Adopting the clothing brand your friends wear, even if you prefer another.
- Taking up a popular hobby at school to feel part of the group.

### Mere-Exposure Effect
Preferring things simply because they are already familiar.
- Buying the brand you've seen advertised, without evidence it's better.
- Feeling more comfortable with and trusting people you've met a few times, even without knowing them well.

### Reactance
Resisting or doing the opposite when you feel your freedom of choice is being restricted or pressure is applied — even against your own interests.
- A teenager refusing on principle to clean their room when parents insist, or even making it messier.

## Behavioral and Memory Biases

### Procrastination
Putting off important tasks despite knowing it will cause problems, filling the time with less important or useless activities.
- Watching series or playing video games instead of preparing for an exam.
- Reading a book or watching YouTube instead of cleaning the apartment.

### Rosy Retrospection (Idealizing the Past)
Remembering the past as better than it actually was — recalling the positives vividly while forgetting or minimizing the negatives.
- Recalling only the fun moments of school while forgetting exam stress and trouble with teachers.
- Older people insisting that in their youth people were kinder, food tasted better, and life was simpler.

## Key Takeaway

These biases share a common thread: the mind takes shortcuts that feel intuitively right but systematically distort reality — inflating our self-image, protecting our existing beliefs, overweighting what's familiar or already invested, and misjudging both other people and our own future. Recognizing the specific pattern at work is the first step toward compensating for it.
