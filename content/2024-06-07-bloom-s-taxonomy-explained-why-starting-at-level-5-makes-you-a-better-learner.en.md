# The Six Levels of Thinking Every Learner Needs to Master

## Overview

Most students struggle to reach top academic results not because they lack intelligence, but because they are **thinking at the wrong level**. This video introduces a framework — Bloom's Revised Taxonomy — that describes six distinct levels of thinking, each unlocking a progressively more powerful type of result. Understanding and deliberately operating at the right level is one of the most effective ways to achieve high academic and professional performance.

---

## The Six Levels Explained

### Level 1: Remember → Result: Regurgitate
- This is the level of **rote learning**: rereading, rewriting, and drilling flashcards repeatedly.
- It feels tedious and often induces mental fatigue.
- The result it produces is the ability to **list, define, and state facts** — the kind of recall-based output that becomes less relevant in higher education and professional life.
- Paradoxically, this level is **not an efficient way to retain information** long-term.

### Level 2: Understand → Result: Explain
- Rather than hammering information into memory through repetition, this level involves genuinely **comprehending what you are learning**.
- Two students can appear to be doing the same thing (e.g., reading a textbook), but their mental intention is different: one is trying to memorize, the other is trying to grasp meaning.
- The result is the ability to **explain concepts and processes**, which makes up the bulk of assessments in early university study.

### Level 3: Apply → Result: Simple Problem Solving
- This level involves **using learned knowledge to solve problems**.
- At this stage, problems are relatively straightforward — often requiring one concept to solve one problem (called **one-to-one problems**).
- Examples include applying a formula in math or physics, or writing a simple function in coding.
- It is reached through regular practice with quizzes and problem sets.

### Level 4: Analyze → Result: Comparison
- This is the entry point into **higher-order thinking**, and it is less commonly reached.
- Level 4 involves **comparing and contrasting** — looking at information in relation to other information, identifying similarities and differences.
- Useful techniques include Venn diagrams, comparison tables, summaries, and mind maps — as long as they force relational thinking.
- Questions at this level require you to examine one concept *against* another, rather than in isolation.
- **Study tip:** Use an AI tool (like ChatGPT or Gemini) with the prompt: *"Give me questions at [year level] for [subject] at Bloom's Revised Taxonomy Level 4."* This generates exactly the types of questions that exam writers use to distinguish good from great learners.

### Level 5: Evaluate → Result: Prioritize
- This is the level that **top learners** operate at, and it is the most impactful for academic and professional success.
- Level 5 goes beyond identifying similarities and differences — it asks **"So what? Why does it matter? What conclusion can be drawn?"**
- You know you are thinking at Level 5 when you are moving back and forth between sources, forming and justifying conclusions, and critically weighing relationships between ideas.
- The same technique (e.g., making a mind map) can be done at Level 3 or Level 5 depending on the **mental process** behind it. A Level 5 mind map involves critically evaluating which connections are most important and why — not just drawing lines between words.
- Level 4 and 5 thinking is required in second and third year university, postgraduate study, and virtually any senior professional role.

### Level 6: Create → Result: Hypothesize
- The highest level involves **synthesizing new, novel ideas** from existing knowledge — identifying a gap and constructing a reasoned hypothesis.
- This is **not** simply generating any output; it is only Level 6 if you are producing something you believe does not already exist in your knowledge base.
- This level is relevant mainly at the **highest tiers of education and professional expertise**, and most people will reach the top of their field by consistently performing at Level 5.

---

## Why Most Students Get Stuck at Lower Levels

- **Misinterpreted effort hypothesis**: When moving from Level 3 to Level 4 and beyond, thinking becomes noticeably harder and slower. Many students interpret this as a sign something is wrong, so they retreat to easier, lower-level strategies — even though the increased effort is a sign of deeper learning.
- **Knowledge decay**: Memory follows a **forgetting curve**. If a student works through levels sequentially (1 → 2 → 3 → …), lower-level knowledge decays while they are working on higher levels, forcing constant relearning and leaving insufficient time to reach Level 5.

---

## The Better Strategy: Start at Level 5 and Work Down

### Why This Counterintuitive Approach Works
- The brain **forms stronger memories and deeper knowledge** when processing information at higher levels of thinking.
- When you aim for Level 5 (evaluation and prioritization), your brain **automatically fulfills the lower levels as a side effect** — you cannot evaluate something without first understanding, applying, and analyzing it.
- The reverse is not true: studying purely to memorize does **not** automatically lead to the ability to compare, evaluate, or prioritize.

### How to Apply This in Practice
1. **Do not begin by trying to memorize or passively understand** new material.
2. **Focus your attention on evaluating** — ask yourself which ideas matter most, how they connect, and why they are significant.
3. Understanding, recall, and problem-solving ability will develop **naturally** as a consequence of this higher-level engagement.
4. Use AI-generated Bloom's Taxonomy questions at Levels 4 and 5 **from the start** to orient your studying toward the right level of thinking.

---

## Key Takeaway

The level at which you *think* — not just the techniques you use — determines the quality of your results. Most students are capable of Level 4 and 5 thinking but default to lower levels out of habit or because difficulty feels like failure. By deliberately targeting higher-order thinking from the beginning of your study process, you learn more deeply, forget less, and unlock the kind of performance that separates top students from the rest.
