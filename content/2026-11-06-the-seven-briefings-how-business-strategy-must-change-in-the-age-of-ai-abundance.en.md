# The Seven Briefings: How the Rules of Business Have Changed

## Why Elite Strategy Advice Now Costs Six Figures

The stakes in business have never been higher. With so much chaos and change in the current environment, the decisions a company makes over the next 12 months can determine the difference between enormous success and outright failure. Sometimes a single insight or counterintuitive decision that competitors fail to make can be the deciding factor. Given stakes of that magnitude, six-figure strategy advice isn't really extravagant — it functions more like insurance. While personalized advice can't be delivered to everyone, the underlying macro patterns can be shared. These patterns are not finished; they are still unfolding and accelerating. Importantly, if strategies that used to work have stopped working, it is not a reflection of declining performance — the ground itself has shifted beneath every business, whether leaders acknowledge it or not.

## Briefing One: It Has Never Been Easier to Start a Business — But Growing One Is a Different Story

The commonly repeated claim that "it has never been easier to start and grow a business" is only half true. Starting is easy; growing is increasingly hard, because supply is exploding everywhere while demand remains essentially flat.

### Evidence of the Supply Explosion
- In the first half of 2026, the number of new apps released on the App Store doubled, with roughly 560,000 new apps launched in six months — while downloads rose only 2% over the same period.
- Music uploads to streaming platforms rose from about 40,000 tracks a day in 2019 to around 150,000 today, with roughly 44% of tracks on Deezer now fully AI-generated.
- Americans filed a record 5.67 million new business applications last year, over 60% more than in 2019.
- Even in heavy manufacturing, supply has exploded: China alone now has around 129 electric car brands (only about 10–11 of which are profitable), with a record 156 new models launching in the second half of this year — compared to roughly 10–11 car brands that existed globally a generation ago.

The core pattern: supply is doubling while demand barely moves. The moment a customer problem appears, dozens of competitors emerge to solve it, and as AI tools get better and cheaper, that number will keep multiplying — from 10 competitors to 20, then 50.

### The Real Lesson: Focus on Supply, Not Just Demand
Business success comes down to supply and demand — increase demand and decrease supply, and profit follows. Most businesses only chase the demand side (more marketing, more leads, more content), but demand has natural limits. If supply keeps outpacing demand, no amount of advertising will fix it. The real question businesses need to ask is: **how do we become something there is less of?** Scarcity, not effort, is what unlocks pricing power.

## Briefing Two: AI Is Turning Scarce Things Into Abundant Things — And Destroying Their Value

A popular but dangerous belief is that AI's ability to "build anything" is great news. In reality, whatever becomes easy, cheap, and abundant instantly loses value. Value doesn't come from inherent quality — it comes from **desirability combined with scarcity**. Water is essential to life yet nearly worthless commercially because it's abundant; the moment scarcity disappears, so does value, regardless of usefulness.

### How AI Has Eroded Value Over the Last Three Years
AI has systematically converted scarce skills into abundant commodities: research, copywriting, analysis, design, software, and expertise were once valuable ("gold") but are increasingly "lead" as each new model release expands the list of commoditized skills.

- A personal example: writing thoughtful LinkedIn posts in 2023 built a following of 130,000 because few people could produce that quality — but the value came from scarcity, not inherent quality, and vanished once AI made similar content trivial to produce.
- A tool built in 20 minutes using AI cannot be sold for serious money, even if it's genuinely good — because anything buildable in 20 minutes has no scarcity value, no matter how useful it is.
- Usefulness and commercial value are not the same thing. The goal should be creating "useless gold" (scarce, high value) rather than "useful water" (abundant, low value).

### Corporate Casualties of This Shift
- **Chegg**, the homework-help company, saw revenue fall 39% in a single year (from roughly $600 million to $377 million) after ChatGPT eliminated the scarcity that justified its $15/month subscription; its stock is now about 99% off its peak.
- The broader "SaaS apocalypse" saw $285 billion wiped off software stocks in a single day (February 3rd) as investors recognized that AI had commoditized many software products — investors update their mental models faster than CEOs do.

### The Exercise to Run
Businesses should audit every source of their perceived value and leverage, sorting them into two categories:
- **Declining leverage**: product/service alone, technology, intellectual property, cost structure, expertise — all becoming easier for everyone and thus less valuable.
- **Rising leverage**: brand, distribution, proprietary data, scarce physical assets — these remain hard to replicate or are becoming even harder to replicate.

Two follow-up questions matter: Is your current advantage sitting on leverage that's eroding (a "melting iceberg")? And how can you build genuine, defensible advantage in one or two of the rising categories — that's where time, talent, and money should go for the next three years.

## Briefing Three: AI and Algorithms Are Converging Everyone Toward the Same Average Answers

Much of today's popular business advice (sell the outcome, niche down, build an irresistible offer, find the pain point) didn't exist 15 years ago. This convergence isn't because anyone cracked the code of business — it's because social media algorithms reward popularity and engagement, not truth or relevance, so the most "clip-friendly" advice wins and everything else gets buried. This homogenizing effect existed before AI, but AI intensifies it because it predicts the statistically most likely (average) answer, trained on an internet already filtered by the same algorithms — a filter on top of a filter.

### Evidence From Research
A Duke University study tested 22 commercial AI models against more than 100 humans on identical creative problems. Individually, AI answers matched human quality, but as a group, AI answers were far more similar to each other than human answers were — even when models were prompted to be more creative, the diversity barely improved.

This matters because **leverage comes from difference, not quality**. Even a weaker analysis can provide an edge if it's different from what competitors are doing. As more of the internet is written by AI, and future models train on that AI-generated content, this convergence will intensify rather than resolve.

### The Hidden Cost: AI Isn't Making Most Companies More Money
MIT research found that 95% of organizations saw no measurable return from generative AI projects, largely because:
1. Companies are using AI to do things cheaper rather than better, which isn't revenue-generating.
2. Since all competitors gain the same AI capabilities simultaneously, nobody gains a relative advantage.

### What to Actually Do
- Use AI only for **closed problems** — tasks with a single right answer (summarizing, checking, building models).
- Avoid outsourcing **open problems** — strategic questions without one correct answer — to AI, since not all valuable knowledge exists on the internet, and AI's convergence effect limits its usefulness here.
- Deliberately **de-converge** personal knowledge: read books (including old or obscure ones), talk to genuine experts motivated by results rather than views, and seek idiosyncratic perspectives. The value isn't that these sources are more correct — it's that they're different, and that difference is the advantage. A personal anecdote illustrates this: two hours of AI-assisted brainstorming on a creative problem went nowhere, but an offhand comment from a spouse with no expertise in the subject sparked the breakthrough, because it introduced genuine randomness into the process.

## Briefing Four: Marketing Has Stopped Working Because Attention Itself Is Collapsing

The belief that solid marketing fundamentals (SEO, ads, email, content) will always generate leads is breaking down because, while nearly everything else is becoming abundant, **attention is shrinking** — and every new content-generation tool accelerates that shrinkage. People have the same 24 hours they've always had, with even less patience, and every brand, creator, and AI-generated piece of content competes for that same finite resource.

### Evidence of Attention Collapse
- In early 2026, 68% of Google searches in the US ended without any click.
- When an AI overview appeared atop search results, the click-through rate for the top organic result dropped by 58% — meaning even a #1 ranking can be functionally invisible.
- Paying for attention is also getting more expensive: Meta's average ad price rose 14% last quarter overall, and 23% in the US and Canada specifically.
- Email is overwhelmed with AI-generated spam, contributing to roughly 392 billion emails sent daily.

### Why Attention Shrinks Rather Than Just Redistributes
When people are overwhelmed by too many options, they don't become better filters — they retreat to a small set of trusted sources (a familiar newspaper, a couple of magazines, a few trusted creators), which causes real, usable attention to contract rather than merely spread thin.

### The New Rule of Marketing
Because so much "good" marketing now exists, it has effectively canceled itself out — if a technique is easy to scale, it will stop working. The marketing that still delivers ROI is **unfair, tedious, or expensive**:

- **Unfair** advantages (uncopyable by competitors) include: fame, a genuinely impressive and verifiable track record, association with recognized names, genuine insight that can't be generated by an LLM, costly signaling, real-world relationships, showmanship, live demonstrations, and physical/offline presence. Checking off at least three of these typically produces an effective way to capture attention.
- **Tedious** marketing means doing what competitors can't be bothered with: original research, hosting your own events, taking on dozens of speaking engagements a year, handwritten letters, and other labor-intensive, low-friction-resistant tactics. In a frictionless world, adding friction becomes the moat.
- **Expensive** marketing works as costly signaling — e.g., a massive billboard takeover in Times Square signals legitimacy precisely because of its cost. A useful test: "If this marketing cost ten times more, would we still be confident doing it?" If not, it's likely low-value "marketing slop."

## Briefing Five: The Age of Business Complacency Is Over

Many businesses rely on long track records and historical stability to assume continued safety, but this complacency is dangerous. For decades, many industries have operated on an unspoken "non-aggression pact" among incumbents — nobody tries too hard, everyone protects their patch, and businesses can thrive on mere mediocrity rather than excellence.

### Why Certain Industries Were Never Disrupted
Industries without a "Netflix of electricians" or "Uber of school photos" weren't protected by any special characteristic of the industry itself — they were protected because they were too tedious or low-ROI to attract the most ambitious, talented entrepreneurs, who instead chased bigger rewards elsewhere. Tedium itself functioned as the moat protecting these industries from aggressive disruption.

## Briefing Six: The Complacent Industries Are About to Be Invaded

### Tedium Was the Moat — Until AI Made It Cheap
AI and easier building/distribution have now made that tedium cheap and easy to overcome. At the same moment, opportunities within software itself are shrinking because large AI companies are absorbing them. The result: a wave of ambitious talent that once wanted to build tech unicorns now has nowhere to go but into sleepy, under-leveraged, complacent industries that have been coasting for decades.

### The Rise of the "Frontier Operator"
This new breed of competitor can be called a **frontier operator** — extremely ambitious, indifferent to work-life balance, winner-take-all in mindset, technologically cutting-edge, and more strategically sophisticated than the average incumbent CEO. These operators don't need to invent anything; they simply need to show up and try far harder than anyone else in a given industry.

- **Case study**: In September of last year, KKR agreed to buy **A1 Garage Door Service** for roughly $2 billion. The founder started with a single van in 2007 and built one of America's largest garage door companies simply by operating at a level the industry had never seen — becoming a frontier operator in a famously unglamorous trade.
- This is described as only the first wave; as tools keep improving, the cost of entering and taking over a sleepy industry will keep falling, meaning more stories like this are coming.

### The Uncomfortable Question Every Business Must Ask
If the main players in an industry have stayed roughly the same for 10–15 years, that is **not** a reassuring sign — it does not mean the business is exceptional or the industry is inherently hard to crack. It simply means ambitious people haven't attacked yet. The analogy offered: playing high school basketball and assuming you don't need to train like an NBA player — until Michael Jordan walks into your gymnasium.

### The Data Confirms a Widening Gap
McKinsey research found that the **top 10% of the world's biggest companies now capture 80% of all economic profit**, a gap that has widened dramatically over the past 20 years due to technology-enabled network effects. The middle 60% of companies make essentially nothing. Winners (consolidators) are pulling away from everyone else.

### The Fix: Become the Frontier Operator in Your Own Space
- The starting point is ambition. Rather than asking "is our strategy good?" (a question people instinctively answer with self-flattering lies), ask: **"What is the realistic chance we grow 3x in the next 18 months?"** This is much harder to lie about.
- If the honest answer is "near zero," that reveals a lack of strategic aggression and vulnerability to disruption.
- Effort alone can only push a business to the ceiling of its current model; growing beyond that ceiling requires building a genuinely different business — which is why big ambition forces real strategy, while modest ambition just permits tinkering.
- A useful thought exercise: imagine a team of ravenous "tech bros" entering your industry tomorrow. How would they attack it, and what would that mean for you? In 2026, the only real form of defense is attack.

## Breaking the Invisible Rules of Your Category

### Why "Being Better" No Longer Wins
The old strategic maxim of beating competitors by doing what they do, just better, has run its course. Trying to be the best player at the existing game now mostly just turns a business into a commodity, especially since competitors can copy and iterate faster than ever. The market does not reward "better" — it rewards **power and difference**. Many superior products have failed simply because they weren't different enough to be noticed.

### Finding Opportunity on the Other Side of the Rules
Every industry runs on a set of invisible, unquestioned rules — how to price, who the customer is, what the product must include, which channels to sell through. Nobody consciously decided these rules; they developed organically and became "wallpaper" that everyone copies without realizing it. In a crowded market, these shared rules are exactly what locks every competitor into sameness. Following the same rules as competitors guarantees landing in the same place they do. New opportunity can only be found by breaking a rule that an entire industry blindly follows.

- **The Savannah Bananas**: A baseball team whose category assumption is "try to win ball games." By questioning that single assumption and asking what would happen if they *didn't* try to win, they instead built the best live fan experience in the sport — filling stadiums (over 50 cities, 81,000 people in one college football stadium last year, 70 major-league stadium stops on this year's world tour requiring a ticket lottery) despite being mediocre at the actual sport. No competing team can copy this without dismantling its own business model.
- **Nintendo Switch**: Won party gaming by sacrificing processing power for portability — a trade-off Sony and Microsoft will never make, because it would undermine their core identity.
- **Build Design Studio** (a small design agency in Birmingham, Alabama): Rather than assuming the agency must act as "guardian" of a client's brand (the industry default), they sell clients the capability to manage their own brand. Within a year of this shift, revenue doubled and profit margin rose tenfold.

### A Practical Exercise for Breaking Rules
1. List your industry's "sacred cows" — the fixed, invisible norms around pricing, channel, customer definition, and features.
2. Identify which of these the business is currently *worst* at, since there's the least to lose there.
3. Ask the deliberately uncomfortable question: "What if we were even worse at this, or didn't do it at all?" and see what becomes possible once that constraint is removed.
4. Most answers generated this way will be bad ideas, but occasionally one will reveal a genuinely defensible, high-leverage opportunity — recognizable because it will feel almost embarrassingly obvious once seen.

## Briefing Seven: Entering a Post-Product World

### Why Great Products No Longer Sell Themselves
The old belief that building an outstanding product is sufficient — and that marketing exists merely to support it — no longer holds. Because nearly anyone can now build and iterate on a product quickly and cheaply, product differences rarely last. Exceptions exist: products with strong network effects (like Instagram), products tied to irreplaceable physical assets (like a hotel on a unique plot of land), and products protected by exclusive licensing. But most businesses have none of these protections, leaving their products fundamentally easy to copy.

### The New Order of Priorities: Attention Before Product
Because attention is now the truly scarce resource, the sequence by which a business wins has flipped:
1. **Attention** is the starting point — every customer has a limited supply, and it flows toward one of two things: things that *attract* them (most discretionary purchases, like a jacket they want) or things that *irritate* them (most non-discretionary purchases, like a broken boiler).
2. That attention must then be converted — attraction is turned into **desire** through brand, story, and content; irritation is turned into **relief** through relevance and ease of access.
3. Only at the end of this chain does the **product** itself matter, and by then its job is simply to live up to the promise already made — the real battle was won earlier, in the customer's mind.

### Evidence of This Shift in the Real World
- This explains why celebrities and influencers constantly launch drinks, skincare, or protein-bar brands: they've already won the attention battle, and people buy wherever that attention is directed — exemplified by "New Tonic," a drink from James Smith and Chris Williamson, which sells not because of its scientific claims but because of who created it.
- In strategy clinics run for members of a paid community, four unrelated businesses (an AI sales tool, a menswear atelier, a book coach, and a solo AI consultant) all converged on the same lesson: value lay not in the product but in attention, distribution, relationship, and trust — exactly the layer that AI makes harder to replicate rather than easier.

### What This Means for Resource Allocation
Businesses are advised to build outward from attention rather than outward from product — functioning more like a media business with a product attached, rather than a product business with media attached. Most companies currently invest their best talent and money into product while leaving attention to junior marketing staff; this allocation should be reversed. This doesn't mean product quality stops mattering — it still must fully deliver — but it is no longer difficult to make a product "good enough." Pouring more resources into marginal product improvements is wasted effort, since competitors will copy any gains almost immediately. The more productive questions are: What is our claim on people's attention? Where is our attention leverage? And where are we directing it?

## Closing Synthesis

All seven briefings share one unifying principle: the need to pull the **supply lever** rather than just the demand lever. This means lowering supply, building leverage in categories of advantage that are rising rather than eroding, deliberately de-converging one's thinking away from AI-driven sameness, making marketing unfair, tedious, or expensive, becoming the frontier operator within one's own industry by challenging a sacred assumption, and building the business outward from attention rather than from product. These approaches are difficult and still genuinely rare across most industries — which is precisely why they remain effective. The core recommendation is to begin applying these principles now, while they are still unusual, rather than waiting until they become standard practice and thus lose their edge.
