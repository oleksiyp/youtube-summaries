# Why Smart People Struggle to Say What They Mean

## The Core Problem: It's Not Your Vocabulary

Understanding something perfectly and being unable to explain it is a common experience. The idea feels whole and clear internally, but when asked to say it aloud, what comes out is disorganized — half-finished sentences, a story that starts in the wrong place, a point that arrives too late or never arrives at all. The common advice is to build a bigger vocabulary and sound more intelligent, but psychological research suggests this is backwards. People who articulate their thoughts clearly typically use **fewer, simpler words**, not more impressive ones.

### The Science Against Big Words

In 2006, psychologist Daniel Oppenheimer tested the popular belief that complex vocabulary makes a person seem smarter. Many students admitted to deliberately inflating their word choice to appear more intelligent, so Oppenheimer ran an experiment: he took pieces of writing and swapped in longer, more impressive words. The result was the opposite of what people expected — the more complex the language became, the **less intelligent** readers judged the writer to be, regardless of whether the underlying writing was good or bad.

This happens because of **processing fluency**: when language is hard to process, listeners feel that friction and unconsciously blame the speaker for it. Big words don't signal intelligence — they signal effort, and listeners read that effort as a performance rather than genuine clarity.

## How Speech Is Actually Built

### Levelt's Three-Stage Model

In 1989, Dutch psycholinguist Willem Levelt proposed an influential model of speech production with three stages:

1. **The Conceptualizer** – where you decide what you actually mean, producing what Levelt called the "preverbal message": a thought that exists before it has been put into language.
2. **The Formulator** – which selects words and arranges grammar.
3. **The Articulator** – which physically produces the sound.

The key insight is that when someone says "I can't find the words," they are blaming the formulator (stage two), but the real breakdown usually happens earlier, in the conceptualizer. Within that first stage lies a process Levelt called **macroplanning**: breaking one big idea into smaller pieces and sequencing them. If this step is skipped, the formulator receives a disorganized pile of ideas instead of an ordered plan — and no amount of vocabulary can fix a message that was never structured in the first place.

### Pinker's Web, String, and Tree

Cognitive scientist Steven Pinker, in *The Sense of Style*, offers a complementary picture using three images:

- **The Web** – how ideas actually exist in the mind: richly interconnected, with every thought linked to several others simultaneously.
- **The String** – what comes out of the mouth: one word after another, in a single line.
- **The Tree** – the structure that converts the web into the string, with a main point as the trunk, supporting points as branches, and details hanging off those branches.

Because thoughts are stored as webs but can only be communicated as strings, the tree-building step — organizing the message before speaking — is the entire game. People who speak with verbal intelligence don't necessarily have richer webs than anyone else; deep thinkers generally do have rich webs. What sets clear communicators apart is that they **build the tree before opening their mouth**.

## The Five Reasons Thoughts Come Out Tangled

### 1. Reaching for the Bigger Word

Under social pressure — such as speaking up in a meeting — people often dress up simple statements with inflated language ("we should leverage and utilize the new framework" instead of "we should use the new system"). This doesn't add meaning; it adds a tax on the listener, who must decode the costume before reaching the idea. Processing fluency means that extra effort gets quietly blamed on the speaker. Worse, fancier words are usually vaguer than plain ones — "leverage" could mean many things, while "use" means one — so precision is lost along with clarity.

### 2. Starting at the Beginning

When asked a direct question like "Is the project done?", many people default to telling the story in chronological order rather than answering directly, because memory naturally stores events in the sequence they happened. But listeners didn't ask for history — they asked for the answer. Every sentence of backstory forces them to hold the point in suspense, which leads to interruptions, listeners finishing sentences, or mentally checking out. The speaker then feels unheard, when in reality the point was simply buried under an unnecessary narrative.

### 3. Speaking the Whole Web at Once

Sometimes one idea triggers a second, which connects to a third, and the speaker tries to include all of it because all of it feels relevant. This is the web trying to escape without having been shaped into a tree. Nelson Cowan's 2001 research on working memory suggests people can only actively hold a small number of information "chunks" at once — estimates generally range from about three to five. When a speaker exceeds that limit, listeners don't receive a richer picture; they receive fragments and retain only whatever piece they happened to catch. Ironically, the deepest, most interconnected ideas are the ones that suffer the most in this kind of delivery.

### 4. Panicking at Silence

A brief pause mid-sentence can feel unbearable, prompting filler words ("um," "like," "basically") or the wrong word chosen in a rush just to keep talking. However, research by psycholinguist Frieda Goldman-Eisler in the 1950s found that hesitations tend to occur right before the least predictable, most information-rich words — meaning a pause often reflects the mind actively selecting precise language, not failing. Psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree argued that filler words like "um" and "uh" function as signals that the speaker is still planning (though the exact extent of this signaling is debated). The real cost comes from panicking at the silence: rushing to fill it sacrifices the precise word for a fast, often wrong one, requiring several follow-up sentences to correct.

### 5. Trying to Say a Thought That Isn't Finished

People often believe they fully understand an idea but simply can't express it — yet frequently what they have is only a *feeling* of knowing, not a completed thought. Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky argued, nearly a century ago, that thought doesn't exist fully formed before language — it is completed **through** the act of putting it into words. This is supported by a 1994 University of Pittsburgh study led by Michelene Chi, in which eighth graders who explained science text to themselves line by line, with no special training, learned significantly more than students who simply reread the same material twice. Explaining an idea aloud doesn't just reveal understanding — it builds it. This means that if the only time someone tries to articulate an idea is during a high-pressure moment (a meeting, interview, or argument), they are doing the finishing work of thinking live, under an audience's gaze — and the resulting frustration gets mistakenly blamed on vocabulary rather than recognized as unfinished thinking.

## The Underlying Paradox

The harder a person tries to sound intelligent, the less intelligent they often sound, and the ideas that matter most — those with the richest connections — tend to come out the worst, because they are the largest webs being forced through a single linear sentence. This is why deep thinkers frequently feel the most misunderstood.

## Three Moves to Speak More Clearly

Common advice — speak more confidently, memorize impressive words, practice sounding polished — is just another version of the "costume" problem. The actual goal isn't more words; it's **better order**.

1. **Say the point first.** Before telling any story, give the one-sentence answer (e.g., "Almost done — one piece is left, and here's why"), and only add backstory if it's requested. This puts the trunk down before the branches. If a one-sentence answer isn't possible, that signals the thought itself isn't finished yet — and it's entirely acceptable to say, "Give me a second, I want to say this well."

2. **Hand listeners a small number.** When making several points, state upfront how many there are (e.g., "there are two reasons"). This forces the speaker to narrow down to what truly matters and tells the listener exactly how much mental space to prepare. When a pause arises mid-sentence, letting it sit communicates careful word choice rather than confusion.

3. **Practice explaining things aloud when nothing is at stake.** Based on the Chi study's findings, this means regularly explaining something already understood — an article, a decision, how something works — out loud to no one in particular (in a car, on a walk, into a voice memo). The first attempts will feel awkward, but this is the point: thoughts get finished in private so they arrive already finished in public. Over time, this builds the habit of constructing the tree before anyone even asks the question.

## The Payoff

This practice doesn't simplify a person's thinking — the underlying web of ideas remains just as rich and interconnected. What changes is the ability to hand that complexity to others one thread at a time, in an order they can actually follow. The shift transforms the dynamic from performing intelligence to genuinely transmitting it: people listen more closely and remember what was said, not because it sounded impressive, but because they were able to follow it all the way through.

## A Closing Thought

In 1674, French poet Nicolas Boileau wrote a line that became a proverb: what is well understood is clearly said, and the words to say it come easily — implying thought precedes language. Centuries later, Vygotsky argued the reverse, that words are what complete a thought. Both views may hold truth simultaneously: thinking leads to speaking, speaking sharpens thinking, and sharper thinking in turn finds cleaner words. This feedback loop — not an innate gift — is what verbal intelligence actually is: a continuous conversation between mind and mouth that improves a little every time it happens.
