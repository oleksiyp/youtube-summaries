# Why the Internet Doesn't Help Us Find the Truth: A Biologist's View

## Introduction and Framing

This livestream tackles a seemingly philosophical question that turns out to be deeply practical: **why unlimited access to information doesn't make people better informed**. The speaker, a biologist, approaches the problem through the lens of how the human "biological computer" actually works — not as moral or social beings first, but as **biological organisms shaped by evolution**. Understanding this, she argues, allows us to build far more realistic relationships with information and how we absorb it.

### A Digression on Free Will

Responding to an audience question, the speaker offers a nuanced position:

- **Free will exists**, but it is heavily constrained by genetics, childhood environment, and the circumstances one currently finds oneself in.
- The key mechanism is the **frontal cortex** — the rational part of the brain — which can recognize that the **limbic system** (responsible for emotions, cravings, and susceptibility to quick rewards and addiction) is misfiring.
- Free will is therefore *not* the ability to transform yourself from a weak-willed person into a strong-willed one. Genetic predispositions don't vanish. Instead, free will means **using the frontal cortex to restructure your environment** so that limbic influences are minimized — for example, simply not keeping sweets at home if you binge on them.
- Believing free will doesn't exist is described as a *convenient* position, because it absolves one of responsibility, but it's the difference between "wanting to actually get somewhere" versus "staying comfortable."

### On the Internet as a Civilizational Turning Point

The speaker notes that almost no science fiction writer predicted the internet's true impact (Sakharov being a rare partial exception). The internet radically changed work, daily schedules (night owls can finally live on their own rhythm), and our entire relationship with information. Yet — and this is the crux — **our biological nature did not change alongside it**.

## The Central Puzzle

Essentially all information is now freely and easily accessible, minus classified material. Anyone can research any question and construct a reasonably objective picture of the world. Yet the opposite happens:

- Enormous numbers of people **do not update their views**; instead they **entrench further**, even in demonstrably false beliefs.
- Examples cited: flat-earth communities (which are growing and holding conferences), and the **vaccines-cause-autism myth**, traceable in three clicks to Andrew Wakefield's falsified, retracted *Lancet* paper — yet the myth "took on a life of its own" and cannot be eradicated.

### Tools Never Changed Human Nature

Experts are only now noticing this, but it was always true. **Books, printing, newspapers, radio, libraries, universal literacy, universal education** — all were expected to transform humanity, and none did. The internet is likewise just another tool. None of these tools can change the one thing that matters: **how the biological component of the mind works**, because that component was formed long before them and is unaffected by them.

## Why Our Brains Resist New Information

### Humility About Human Nature

The speaker argues against the arrogant view that humans are primarily their own creation — the notion that reading good books makes you a good person and watching TikTok makes you degenerate. In reality:

- We are **overwhelmingly the product of biology**, shaped over hundreds of millions of years of evolution.
- Only a tiny fraction of us is the self-authored, conscious "Homo sapiens" layer.

She cites Ivan Yefremov's ***Andromeda Nebula*** as a perfect example of the fallacy — a novel so false-ringing that it becomes instructive. Its premise is that once technology eliminates mundane domestic problems, civilization will spontaneously become a society of scientists, poets, and artists. It feels utterly fake precisely because it **completely ignores human biological nature**.

### Why Evolutionary Mechanisms Can't Be Switched Off

The mechanisms governing our brains were forged by **brutal natural selection where the price of error was death**. Because selection was so harsh, these mechanisms became deeply embedded and **always operate, whether we want them to or not**. Individuals in whom they switched off simply didn't survive. Now that living conditions have softened, these mechanisms keep running unchanged — and often against us.

## Reason One: Natural Selection Has Been Diluted

Our developed frontal cortex built a civilization with social, medical, and technical systems that **help those who would not otherwise survive** to survive.

- The speaker is explicit that this **humanism is correct and is what we should do as people** — helping the sick, the socially disadvantaged, and discriminated minorities.
- But objectively, it means **selection pressure on health has largely stopped**. Previously, weaker individuals died in childhood, and the population maintained high general health; women who couldn't withstand repeated childbirth died, while the strong bore five children.
- *Aside on immunity:* people who catch every cold don't necessarily have "weak immunity." Genetics determine how well certain receptors detect common viruses like rhinovirus; those with better-functioning receptors mount faster immune responses and rarely get sick.

### Selection Against Maladaptive Behavior Has Also Stopped

Survival depended on behavior, not only health. Historically, a lazy, quarrelsome, or unreliable person would be expelled from the group, which meant certain death. Today:

- People with clearly **maladaptive behavior** (heavy drinking, addictions, inability to hold a job) are not filtered out.
- Worse, they often **reproduce more successfully** than people with adaptive behavior — those who build careers, learn languages, plan carefully, and consequently postpone having children.

## Reason Two: The Environment Has Become Radically More Complex

In ancestral environments, responses were to **immediate stimuli**: you see a tiger, you run; you see food or an attractive mate, you approach. Feedback was instant, so learning and selection worked.

Modern society, by contrast, is built on complex laws, intermediaries, and **long delays between action and consequence**:

1. **Credit and consumption** — You want the new iPhone but can only afford the case. A bank or microloan company hands you money instantly, with "nothing to pay right now" and the interest rate in fine print. The consequences arrive far later.
2. **Representative democracy** — You vote for a party over one issue (say, green policy), the party later pushes something you never wanted, and ten years on you swing to a radical alternative. The speaker points to Germany's recent political shift as an example of an outcome everyone predicted yet everyone found shocking.

Because there is **no immediate feedback and no immediate threat to life**, two things fail:
- **Biological selection** for better behavior no longer operates.
- **Individual learning** doesn't occur either, since the link between action and outcome is invisible.

## The Result: Simple Responses to Complex Signals

Our brain receives the **complex signals of the modern world and answers them as if they were simple signals from the savanna**, because that is the only thing it knows how to do. Emotional, immediate reactions that were once adaptive are now frequently inappropriate responses to far more complicated situations.

The most troubling consequence: because these responses are **simple and highly predictable**, they can be deliberately exploited. Radical parties and extreme ideologues consistently use emotionally charged messaging, pressing specific psychological "buttons" to provoke the reliable ancestral reaction. Some manipulators do this intuitively; others do it with full theoretical knowledge — the speaker names **Edward Bernays**, the cynical "father of modern PR and propaganda," as the archetype.

His book was studied closely by **Goebbels**, who used its principles to design anti-Jewish campaigns. Bernays — himself Jewish — found this deeply unpleasant but reasoned that a tool is just a tool, and this one had simply fallen into the hands of people with bad intentions.

Whether educated in the theory or acting on intuition, manipulators know which buttons to press. The consequence: they package their messages so that **our brains perceive them as far more relevant** than messages that would give us an accurate picture of reality. We react to the manipulative signal and ignore the objective one.

This leads to a **third problem**, developed later: **the vast majority of people don't actually need a true picture of the world at all.**

## Why Humans Invent Explanations: The Origin of Worldviews

### Ancient People and the Gods

The internet appeared to solve an old problem — most people's lack of access to adequate information. We can understand why ancient peoples performed human sacrifices and burned witches: they didn't know how natural law worked, so they explained the unexplainable using the only knowledge they had.

Crucially, **they assumed nature operated by the same rules as human relationships**. Look at any mythology — Norse, Greek, Roman, Slavic, Australian, Polynesian — and the gods all have rich, complicated social lives: quarrels, love affairs, betrayal, revenge. Human dynamics were simply transposed onto Olympus or Valhalla.

Two reasons this happened:

- **Innovation requires an existing foundation of knowledge.** New explanatory frameworks don't appear from nothing; they emerge only once enough evidence accumulates to show the old model fails.
- **The mind cannot tolerate "for no reason."** Rain, floods, forest fires, decades-long climate shifts — for prehistoric people these had no discernible cause. Living without understanding the rules governing your surroundings is intensely uncomfortable.

### Prediction as a Survival Mechanism

If we don't understand causes, we cannot predict events; if we cannot predict, we cannot act to minimize risk and maximize benefit. So the brain is **wired to detect causal patterns within seconds**, and it does so not in the frontal cortex through deliberate reasoning but through **much older, faster, automatic mechanisms** working by analogy and shortcut. Those in whom these mechanisms failed did not survive — which is why they now run automatically in everyone.

## Evidence From Infancy

These mechanisms are active from the first weeks of life, long before speech:

- **The object-substitution experiments:** an infant is shown a toy, it passes behind a screen, and a different object emerges. Babies stare much longer at the unexpected object (a blue car replacing a green duck) than when the same object reappears. They cannot speak or consciously reason, yet they register a violation of the rules they've already inferred.
- **Anomalous trajectories:** a ball rolling out from behind a barrier along an impossible path also produces prolonged staring.
- **The visual cliff:** babies who can crawl refuse to cross a sheet of plexiglass spanning two tables. They see transparency, expect to fall, and are astonished when they don't — much like adults on a skyscraper's glass floor.

The conclusion: **infants a few weeks old already possess a working model of the world**, built unconsciously, allowing them to orient and predict the results of their actions.

### Dopamine and the Drive to Explore

Children are far more curious than adults because **dopamine — the anticipation-of-pleasure neurotransmitter — rewards the acquisition of new information**. This is what makes an infant pull the tablecloth, poke a fork into an outlet, or push over a vase or aquarium. It isn't mischief; it's an evolved, selected-for research program aimed at rapidly learning how the world works: what falls, what burns, what's hot, what's cold. It also demands enormous vigilance from parents — the process is a **joint effort between mother and infant**.

If the world worked differently — if objects fell upward — the child would learn that instead. But to learn the actual rules, one must test them.

The speed is remarkable. The speaker compares it to **Neo in *The Matrix*** being uploaded with kung fu: from a newborn who knows nothing to a one- or two-year-old who walks, talks, and understands slippery, soft, and rough — a genuine phase transition, driven by the burden of our large brain's need for information.

### Modern Signals Confusing the Process

A telling modern artifact: **children trying to pinch-zoom on window glass**, having learned the gesture from tablets and phones. Watching from a fifteenth-floor apartment, a child tries to magnify the view and fails.

The speaker rejects the alarmist conclusion. Children adapt quickly and simply learn that windows work differently. More importantly, **withholding phones and tablets from children is genuinely harmful** — not because they should use them all day, but because phones, tablets, the internet and IT are now inseparable parts of the world. Denying children fluency in them **voluntarily strips them of competitive advantages** other children will have.

## Why Curiosity Shuts Down With Age

By adolescence, exploratory behavior largely stops — the teenager lies on the sofa with a phone, chats with friends, plays football, and wants nothing more. This too has a reason:

- Curiosity is **dangerous**, as every parent of an infant knows; the longer it persists, the greater the risk.
- Curiosity **costs energy and time**. In nature, energy is never spent on what brings no benefit.
- Historically, **the world barely changed** across years, centuries, even millennia. So the adaptive strategy was: build a picture of the world quickly, then stop expending effort and simply **stay loyal to that picture**, since it would remain valid for life.

## Defending the Worldview

Because a stable world made worldview-stability adaptive, **selection favored mechanisms that protect the worldview from outside influence** — and those mechanisms still function extremely well today.

When someone or something (a person, or simply the unfolding of events — the thousandth "warning bell" being ignored) presents data that would destroy our picture of the world, the brain **resists with everything it has**, and in our case it does so through **emotion**.

### What Emotions Are, Biologically

Emotions are **markers by which the brain flags what matters**:

- Something useful for survival → **positive emotion** → dopamine → "we need to get this" → action to acquire it.
- Something harmful → **negative emotion** → unpleasant sensation → "stay away from this." (Taste something bitter and unpleasant, and you won't try it again.)

Since a threat to one's worldview was always dangerous, we experience **negative emotions toward whatever or whoever threatens it**, and we keep our distance accordingly.

## The Cognitive Biases That Result

The two drives — building a single coherent worldview and defending it — express themselves as a family of cognitive biases. The most important in this context:

### 1. The Familiar Is Perceived as True

If we encounter a piece of information many times, we unconsciously begin to accept it as true, or at least normal. The logic is ancestral: **you walked past that bush a hundred times and no tiger jumped out, so the bush is safe.**

This applies equally to ideas and news:
- **Emotional habituation:** the first news of war is shocking, drawing on every film and book you know; by the tenth time you're scrolling past, bored, wanting celebrity news or football instead.
- **Factual contamination:** hear once that an anti-corruption opposition figure is a thief and you dismiss it; hear it a tenth time from different channels and doubt creeps in — "no smoke without fire." Fake-news producers exploit precisely this, and smoke without fire is easy to manufacture.

This bias operates independently of our will and must be **constantly monitored**.

### 2. Selective Perception

Information confirming our worldview is **absorbed faster, remembered better, and retrieved more easily** — demonstrated repeatedly in experiments. Evolutionarily this makes sense: reinforcing an existing model makes it more complete and improves responses to incoming signals. Propaganda exploits this by attaching arbitrary claims to identities you already hold ("you're proud of your country, aren't you? — then this action increases that pride").

### 3. Perceptual Bias (Confirmation Bias, Strong Form)

Information that **contradicts** our worldview seems *a priori* less convincing. Show a flat-earther a globe or a satellite photograph and they'll declare it Photoshop — "I can literally see where the bottom was edited." The bias blocks perception of anything destructive to the model, because **being left without a worldview means being left without a prediction tool**, which is terrifying.

### 4. Cherry-Picking

The process compounds: we begin actively ignoring contradicting news and selecting confirming news from the stream. The brain is doing what it considers maximally useful — **preserving your worldview**.

### 5. The Time Factor

The longer you marinate in inaccurate information, the deeper it penetrates — hearing something twice differs from hearing it 122 times from multiple sources; **quantity becomes quality**.

**Age matters too.** A well-known study (often cited by Dima Zitser) found that children who attended primary school in Nazi Germany retained antisemitic attitudes for life at a far higher rate than people exposed to the same propaganda at other ages. The reason: exposure hit the **sensitive period** during which the worldview is actively being constructed.

A related everyday example: children are convinced their parents are the smartest people alive, which is why the adolescent discovery that they aren't can be such a painful, psyche-straining disillusionment.

### 6. Wishful-Thinking Bias

We accept information that is **pleasant** to us more readily, because for the brain **pleasant equals useful** — the same mechanism behind problems with junk food and alcohol. The frontal cortex may know better, but the limbic system remains convinced.

Hence the effectiveness of flattery — the classic fable of **the crow and the fox**. A person who tells you how clever and wonderful you are seems far more agreeable than a blunt truth-teller attacking head-on. Clichés about loyalty to one's company, country, or duty to the motherland work beautifully for exactly this reason.

## The Wall of Beliefs

Together these biases mean you filter the objective information stream down to whatever reinforces your existing model, building a **wall of convictions** that is very hard to breach. Attempts to breach it produce irritation and hostility.

### Real-World Consequences

- **The cancer patient:** unexplained fatigue, persistent pain, obvious warning signs — but the fear is so great that everything is explained away as stress and overwork, until internal bleeding from a tumor forces the issue and the diagnosis is stage three or four. The unbearable part was admitting that the worldview "I am healthy and invulnerable" was false.
- **Latent homosexuality** and other conflicts touching on intimate life follow the same pattern of prolonged self-blinding, which grows harder to reverse over time.
- **Living amid repression:** if you're afraid and cannot leave, it's terrifying to accept that you live among murderers. So people rationalize — "under Stalin the enemies of the people really were enemies; Comrade Stalin just made a monstrous mistake." The brain will invent any explanation to **stretch reality over the existing model** rather than let it collapse, because collapse is genuinely frightening for the psyche.

**Recommended viewing:** ***The Truman Show*** — precisely about the difficulty of discovering that everything is not as you believed.

## How New Media Made It Worse

One would expect open access to improve matters. It hasn't.

### Historically, Exposure Was Minimal

Living in a village meant no newspapers, no television — you knew only what happened locally. Even after mass media appeared, many people didn't read papers or listen to radio, and this persisted for decades before the internet. Most people simply lived within the affairs of themselves and their acquaintances; large-scale news barely reached them.

Throughout history, **the overwhelming majority of people had no idea what was happening in the world**. Only the elite — the genuinely privileged part of society, monarchs corresponding with foreign courts — had that picture. For everyone else, information meant a town crier announcing something in the square.

### Today: Total Immersion

Information noise is everywhere; **avoiding it now requires real effort**. Boredom drives this: boredom is uncomfortable, we rarely tolerate it for more than a few seconds, and we escape into the internet — Facebook, Instagram, a news site. Even entering a VKontakte group, a forum, or a WhatsApp chat, news and politics seep in.

Never in history have such enormous masses of people been so densely immersed in information flows.

The critical asymmetry: **human biology has not changed** — people always reacted to information this way — but previously there was far less information, and far less incentive to manipulate ordinary people who knew nothing anyway. Manipulation operated at other levels. Now, through public television, radio, and the internet, it reaches **every single person directly**.

## If We Defend Our Worldview, How Does Progress Happen?

An obvious objection arises: if our brains are built to protect an existing picture of the world, how do humans ever develop new ideas? Biology has an answer.

### Neoteny: Humans as Perpetual Juveniles

**Neoteny** is a well-documented biological phenomenon — normally discussed in arthropods, worms, amphibians and many plants — in which an organism becomes sexually mature while still at an early developmental stage (for instance, reproducing as a larva rather than as an adult imago).

There is a hypothesis that humans are so successful precisely because we have retained a form of neoteny: **we keep the curiosity that in other animals disappears after infancy.**

- Kittens chase yarn and feathers; adult cats mostly sleep. Puppies play; adult dogs vary, but dogs are a special case, having been profoundly reshaped by human influence.
- In the wild — lion cubs, bear cubs, fox kits — the young play constantly, while adults essentially stop. Play is a **learning mechanism** through which juveniles build their model of how the world works. Once the model is built, play becomes an unnecessary expenditure.

### The Neurobiology Behind It

This difference is not metaphorical but measurable:

- In juveniles, the **limbic system and dopamine circuits** — everything associated with learning — are far more active.
- In adults, the limbic system quiets down and the **frontal cortex** takes over (though in many people not especially well).
- In humans, the frontal cortex only finishes developing around age **21**, which is why the adolescent brain is genuinely different from the adult brain.

### The "Program" Takes Over

Once the worldview is built, a different program switches on — the same one that governs all living things: **reproduce and defend resources**. That is the point of evolution: to push one's genes through the generations. Curiosity would only get in the way of executing this program.

So the sequence is: in childhood, use brain plasticity and curiosity to absorb behavioral strategies and construct a model of the world; then switch to reproduction and resource defense.

## Adolescents as Carriers of New Ideas

Because teenagers have not yet activated the adult program, their **worldview-construction machinery is still running at full power**. This is precisely where societal development comes from.

- Throughout history and literature, the enthusiastic champions of new ideas — communism, nihilism, any ideology whatsoever — have been young people.
- The same is true today: Fridays for Future, Free Palestine, BLM. Whatever the idea, its hottest proponents are overwhelmingly the young.
- They are also more temperamental, because the limbic system is running at full throttle while the prefrontal cortex is not yet complete.

An aside on how prolonged modern "childhood" has become: in Germany, parents are expected to fund education up to age 26 — an age at which a person could easily already have three children of their own.

### Why Not From Textbooks?

If teenagers are so eager to absorb new information, why do they soak up nonsense from their peer environment — passionately defending slogans while not even knowing which river or which sea is being referred to — rather than the carefully vetted knowledge in school textbooks?

The technical answer: **there were no textbooks in the caves.** We never evolved neurophysiological mechanisms that reward the absorption of information from textbooks. We *can* learn from them, but it requires extra, deliberate effort, because there is no built-in reinforcement. For other sources — the ones that actually existed in our ancestral environment (which, strictly speaking, was the savanna rather than caves) — the reinforcing mechanisms are firmly in place.

## The Brain Cares Deeply About the Source

This produces another problem for objective information and fact-checking: **our brain is not indifferent to where information comes from** — but not in the disciplined, source-critical way a fact-checker would hope. Almost the opposite.

### Authority as the Ancient Filter

Information from a person we respect, or from an influencer with a million subscribers, is *a priori* perceived as more credible. Historically, information came from watching the elders of the tribe, the leader of your adolescent gang, or the old man showing you how to make pottery or hunt. Children believe their parents for the same reason — which is why deceiving children is so damaging: the discovery of the deception is a serious stress.

The evolutionary logic: if we treated information from any member of our tribe — or a neighboring tribe — as equally valid, we would not have survived. Some **sorting criterion** was needed, and for millennia that criterion was **authority**, which is itself sustained emotionally: fear of the person, love for them, or the more complex emotion of respect.

### When Authority Betrays

This explains a familiar, deeply uncomfortable experience:

- A beloved children's doctor — a profession surrounded by an entire halo of warm associations — suddenly starts spouting fascist nonsense.
- A favorite actor who played noble characters, or a rock musician who preached admirable ideas, says something that sends a chill through your body.

It is extremely difficult to separate the person from the trusted image. This is not an illusion — it is the ancient mechanism working exactly as designed. Information from authorities is absorbed more easily, and historically this was right more often than wrong, which is why it became entrenched.

### The Majority as an Authority

A second source, selected for over millions of years, is **information supported by the majority**. If the majority does something and has survived, it must be a correct behavioral pattern.

There is a second, punitive mechanism reinforcing this: society defends its collective picture of the world against outsiders who threaten it — with the combined force of the majority, utterly incommensurate with the strength of one individual. Going against the majority therefore means a high probability of **ostracism and rejection by the group**, which in ancestral conditions meant death. A handful of Robinson-Crusoe survivors can be safely subtracted from the equation as statistical error.

Crucially, this calculation does not happen consciously. Nobody sits and reasons, "I live in a country where everyone supports the regime, so I'd better agree." It happens in the **subconscious calculator beneath the prefrontal cortex**, running on analogies, sensations and limbic input, and it very quickly concludes that opposing the majority is dangerous.

## Cognitive Dissonance and How We Suppress It

To conform with a majority whose views contradict our own, we deploy mechanisms aimed at combating **cognitive dissonance** — the acutely unpleasant state in which our behavior contradicts our self-image. ("I thought I was a good, decent person, yet here I stand with blood dripping from my hands.") It is painful because it destroys the most fundamental part of the worldview: *who am I in this world?*

When the majority supports cannibalistic ideas that clash with our inner convictions, the frontal cortex invents justifications:

- **Blaming the victim** — the classic move. We clearly perceive an injustice and are highly sensitive to it, but since we feel we can do nothing, it is easier to conclude "she had it coming" or "he brought it on himself."
- **Dehumanization** — constructing a durably negative image of the target of the majority's aggression ("horrible orcs" and so on), so as not to feel sympathy.

We don't calculate this on paper; we *feel* it. The limbic system works out, before the frontal cortex engages, that sympathizing with this person is not in our interest: it will produce either internal turmoil or expulsion from the group — both catastrophic.

### Why Abstract Morality Loses

Why do we contrive ways to follow the majority's monstrous views rather than defending abstract moral values? Because **the values are moral and abstract, while rejection by the majority is entirely real**. The consequences of publicly defending high-minded positions are concrete: prison sentences and repression under a harsh regime; under a milder one, social condemnation, strange looks, no longer being invited anywhere.

It may seem trivial that people look at you funny — but it isn't. **Humans are acutely social, socially hypersensitive creatures.** Society is the second great factor, alongside the brain, behind our evolutionary success. We are poorly built animals — no claws, no fangs, no gills, no wings — yet we conquered the planet more thoroughly than some viruses, thanks to our brains and our social organization. Even the most committed introverts are profoundly dependent on the group. When it feels like you desperately don't want society's condemnation, that feeling is accurate: your brain is generating a mass of negative emotion at the very prospect.

## Why a Fake in the WhatsApp Chat Beats a Sourced Article

All of this explains a common asymmetry:

- A fake news item forwarded by another mother into your parents' WhatsApp group carries **more weight** than a long, carefully verified fact-checking article with an extensive source list.
- The fake is heard **repeatedly** — in the parents' chat, the drivers' chat, the badminton group, over beers with friends — and repetition breeds acceptance.
- The fact-checking article arrives from a distant acquaintance or, worse, from someone you are arguing with online, who says "argue properly — here's an article." That person is effectively an **enemy attacking your picture of the world**. The source is therefore *a priori* non-authoritative, the content is immediately perceived as less credible, and selective perception and cherry-picking continue to operate — as they always do, given how brutal the selection pressure behind them was.

## Do People Even Want an Objective Picture of the World?

### Fact-Checkers Are Unusual People

Fact-checkers and those who insist on verifying everything genuinely need an accurate worldview — because for them, **the act of verification is pleasurable**. Put them in a scanner while they confirm a hypothesis in some article and you would see pleasure-related brain regions light up. Because of how their minds are wired, digging through data is enjoyable, which is why they make it their profession or a serious hobby.

This is generally how people choose work: in line with what gives them pleasure. A careless person shouldn't be an accountant (they'd quickly be jailed for embezzlement); someone who loves order and neat figures will thrive in that role.

### The Uncomfortable Truth About Everyone Else

**The majority of people do not need an objective picture of the world — more precisely, they are not interested in it.** They are broadly indifferent to objectivity, and care about other things:

- Family
- Sports
- Simply a calm existence with whatever comfort is available at their level: TV series, a glass of wine, a beer with friends at the bar
- "We only live once — I just want to enjoy life without straining, and drift with the current"

People have jobs, domestic obligations, taxes. They have no interest in delving into things that seem abstract and remote. And, once again, this is reinforced by evolved mechanisms: **understanding a distant conflict brings no immediate result.** This is the same reason quitting smoking or dieting is so hard — where's the payoff after two days on a diet? Abstract questions about who is right and wrong in some conflict, who started what, feel so remote and dull that people fall into a kind of trance.

The speaker offers a personal parallel: professional sports produce exactly this trance state in her — a hypnotic blankness, like a Kashpirovsky séance. Not because she thinks sport is useless (it's an enormous, perfectly worthwhile entertainment industry), but because it simply doesn't interest her. Everyone has such topics; for other people they might be justice, science, or astronomy.

### The Deliberate Cultivation of Political Apathy

The refusal to engage with politics is particularly dangerous, especially when **authorities actively work to cultivate it** — pushing the image that politicians are all either freaks or people hungry for fame and money. Experienced people who understand these technologies don't merely say "politics is bad"; they **construct the emotional sensation** that politics is repellent. This always ends badly.

### The Berlin Bunker Museum

At the Hitler bunker museum in Berlin, alongside photographs of Kristallnacht, hangs statistics on Jewish emigration from Germany before and after that night. Two figures were striking:

1. **How few left beforehand** — a substantial number in absolute terms, but far fewer than the size of the diaspora living there, even though the situation was visibly deteriorating and aggression was escalating month by month.
2. **How few left even afterward** — after the pogroms of Jewish shops and businesses made everything obvious. For some, that was the alarm bell they finally heard: they packed a suitcase and left. But we know how many stayed, and we know what happened to them.

They stayed because of cognitive dissonance and the invention of justifications — "yes, it's happening, but it doesn't concern me" — driven by the desire to preserve a positive outlook.

### The Optimism Bias

This is another dangerous distortion: **we live more easily believing everything will be fine.** If we accurately calculated our odds of failure, we might simply jump out the window, because realistically each individual's chances of success are very small. Only our failure to grasp how small they are keeps us enthusiastic and moving forward.

So people shut their eyes to obvious warning signals — even to a tocsin — because their brains are shielding them from the destruction of their worldview. In the German case, leaving meant abandoning an entire life, and conditions elsewhere were plainly bad: many countries refused Jewish refugees, ships were turned back, people ended up in Palestine, America, wherever they could. Emigration was genuinely a hard choice — but weighing all inputs would have shown it was the right one. It contradicted the picture of the world and promised discomfort, so many chose blindness.

The general principle: our mechanisms were never adapted to **complex political signals**, which did not exist in ancestral environments. When such complex signals appear, our machinery malfunctions and we make objectively inadequate decisions.

## How the Internet Actively Reinforces Delusions

It gets worse. The internet — a repository of information about everything — not only fails to help people establish the truth, it **entrenches their errors**, precisely *because* it contains so much information about everything. Anyone convinced of something false ("the Earth is flat") will have no trouble finding supporting material.

### Verification Isn't the Goal

Most people are utterly indifferent to whether information is reliable. They don't know how to check it and don't want to — because **if they've found something confirming their view, that was the goal all along**: to reinforce their position, not to obtain an accurate picture of the world.

### The Old Tricks Still Work

Most people fall for the effective techniques that make information feel more relevant — the long-known propaganda methods described by Bernays: **playing on emotions, invoking authorities, appealing to the majority.**

This is why grotesque conspiracy sites — UFOs, flat earth, anti-vaccine material — enjoy such wild popularity. To an outsider it looks like nonsense with eye-searing design, but the design is irrelevant: **the authors press the emotional buttons correctly and offer simple answers to complex questions.** Conspiracy theories map beautifully onto how our brains work, not least because they supply someone to blame.

### The Burden of an Accurate Worldview

By contrast, the objective picture is **always complex, always full of a million nuances**. Genuinely holding to it requires accepting that:

- There is no absolute good and no absolute evil.
- Everyone is capable of behaving badly and frequently does — even those on the side of good sometimes do appalling things.
- Despite all of this, you must still make moral choices and act.

You must operate in a world of greys rather than blacks and whites while simultaneously maintaining high moral standards — which massively complicates decision-making and the evaluation of others' behavior.

## The Technical Problem: People Can't Search

Readers of the speaker's Telegram channel raised an additional, purely practical obstacle in the comments to the stream announcement: **most people have no idea how to search the internet.**

They formulate queries like *"Explain why vaccines cause autism"* or simply *"vaccines autism proof"* — if they can manage keywords at all. Naturally, such phrasing leads them straight to precisely the sites described above, where they instantly find confirmation. And from there the process compounds: once reinforced, the picture becomes progressively harder to dismantle.

Ask them "what kind of source is that, anyway?" and they simply don't care. **Source criticism is a vital discipline that interests no one except scientists and fact-checkers.** Everyone else was told something vivid, emotional and beautiful; your "if A then B" reasoning about some murky methodological point just triggers the trance state and they stop listening.

## We Are Not Computers Running on Silicon

The view that people who fail to seek out truth are simply "lazy or malicious" is a robot's view of humanity. You can load a great deal of information into a machine and it may or may not extract a picture of the world from it — but either way, **our brains do not run on silicon chips**.

One of the key mechanisms of our biological computer is **emotion**. We evaluate all incoming information through the prism of emotions, which historically helped us distinguish good from bad and react accordingly — and which we then use to construct the picture of the world that we go on to defend so fiercely.

### The Cult of Facts Is a Recent Invention

Modern civilization has a **cult of facts**, and facts genuinely are important for science, technology, medicine and progress. This has produced a widespread conviction that facts now govern our thinking. **They do not, and never did.**

- We evolved under completely different conditions. Facts are a brand-new invention.
- In the ancestral environment there were only **simple signals** requiring immediate reactions — no complex, abstract facts.
- The appearance of facts in enormous quantities, and their importance today, has not changed how we function. If anything, the opposite: by creating civilization we launched a host of **supra-biological processes** no longer governed by natural selection, and we stopped changing physiologically. We are simply **not adapted to this new reality**, still operating as we did in the caves, when signals were simple, responses instant, and emotions reliable guides to behavior.

### The Practical Conclusion

If we want to build a better world grounded in objective facts, we must quickly accept that **people are not computers** and learn to account for this factor rather than trying to change it.

Many people dislike this fact and try to batter their way through it, as if pressing a little harder would make their own brain — and everyone else's — start working differently. It won't. Instead of trying to break through a wall with your forehead and pound people with facts, **information must be delivered in a form people actually want to consume.**

### The Forces of Evil Have Mastered This Art

Unfortunately, this art has largely been mastered by **propagandists, populists, and politicians pursuing short-term aims**. They are vitally interested in delivering memorable messages precisely because they need to conceal the truth: what they say and what they actually intend are two different things, so they want people to search as little as possible for the reality behind the message.

The "forces of good," meanwhile, keep insisting that **fact-checking is the supreme virtue** — a god to be prayed to — while ignoring how minds actually work.

## The Education System's Failure to Understand Human Nature

A separate and glaring example of misunderstanding human nature is the modern education system.

### The Myth of Natural Interest

Western education is built on the idea that if we stop pressuring children, they will inevitably start doing what interests them. The results — a **collapse in PISA scores across Europe**, especially in the Scandinavian countries with their supposedly wonderful (in fact monstrous) systems — show otherwise.

The biological reason: **most people are not particularly interested in anything.**

- For survival, a **moderate interest in everything** was entirely sufficient, so that is exactly how our psychology works.
- There was no selection pressure for every child to be especially talented at or fascinated by something. Such traits were unnecessary for survival, so no selection went in that direction.
- The romantic idea that every person has some special hidden interest is false. Most people find everything moderately interesting or moderately uninteresting.

### What Happens Without Guidance

If children are not directed — and sometimes compelled, though skillfully, combining carrot and stick and varying the pressure so as not to provoke revulsion — they will gravitate toward **the easiest activities and quickest pleasures**, because their brains push them there. Only a handful will voluntarily study higher mathematics or anything demanding.

### Why Gamified Learning Breaks Down

The heavy emphasis on **game-based delivery** fails for the same reason: children happily play while it stays easy. But the moment they must move from play to something serious — geometry problems, or problems with parameters, anything harder than coloring a circle in your favorite color — real effort is required, it stops resembling a game, and interest evaporates instantly.

The consequence: children emerge from such systems **without any firm, coherent picture of the world**, which makes them extremely easy to influence. The system effectively ensures no clear worldview forms, because it lets them avoid engaging deeply with anything.

### But Old Systems Weren't the Answer Either

This is one more factor reinforcing the gloomy conclusion that the internet doesn't help establish truth — and may even entrench existing convictions — while education helps by leaving many people without clear convictions at all.

Still, the generation raised in the **Soviet school**, which used entirely different methods, proved just as susceptible to black magic, faith healers like Kashpirovsky, and similar nonsense. So the question is genuinely hard: how to design a system that accounts both for how the brain works and for modern norms — we cannot return to universal corporal punishment — while avoiding purely populist fixes.

## Summary of the Core Argument

The main thesis restated: **do not count on the internet to reveal the truth**, just as it was wrong to count on libraries or universal literacy before it.

- The brain defends whatever picture of the world it has already formed, which makes it critical that the picture form correctly **from the start** — and it usually doesn't.
- The further along one goes, the harder that wrong picture becomes to change.
- It is irrelevant that all the necessary information is one or three clicks away, available at any level of complexity. **Information does not enter the brain on its own.** We would need to create behavior that stimulates searching for it — and there are no biological preconditions for such behavior.

## Audience Questions and Answers

### Should Critical Thinking and Source Evaluation Be Taught in Schools?

Yes — these subjects should be brought back, along with **logic**, though the questions of which grade, school versus university, and whether to repeat them remain open.

The bigger question is **how** to teach them:

- At university, the speaker had **bioethics** — a course everyone skipped, copying notes from the few diligent students the night before the exam. Yet once you actually work in science, you realize bioethics (and biopolitics) are enormously important: they govern how animals may be used in experiments, what suffering is or isn't justified and why. Because everyone skipped them, actual knowledge in this area is dismal.
- If subjects are taught formally and dully, introducing them changes nothing.
- The Russian school system suffered from **total separation of theory and practice**: physics and mathematics had no relation to each other, appearing as two separate difficult subjects tormenting children. Subjects should be taught jointly so students see how abstract algebraic formulas apply to real processes in life, or how physics overlaps with biology. Otherwise it all feels like disconnected chunks of useless abstraction that teachers simply dump on you.
- **Logic** was taught in the pre-revolutionary Russian school and later removed. It matters because propaganda and unscrupulous debaters deliberately use **logical fallacies**. Once you know them, you spot them instantly — the classic **false dichotomy** ("either we reform education or we all die") fools many people, and subtler fallacies exist too.

The deeper rationale: **critical thinking, source evaluation, and logic all strengthen the prefrontal cortex**, helping it restrain the limbic system and more quickly notice manipulation — whether from your own limbic system rescuing you from cognitive dissonance, or from someone manipulating you from above.

Implementing this would require assembling many experts (and determining who counts as an expert is its own problem) to design a genuine teaching methodology.

### Could We Understand Aliens If We Can't Formulate Our Own Thoughts?

Who says aliens will think at all? The speaker maintains — as argued in an earlier video — that **we will never meet aliens**, for objective reasons rooted in the laws of physics. It is a purely theoretical question.

We might see traces of alien life on other planets through telescopes, but most likely these will be **single-celled organisms**. Any life presumably passes through stages, and the only life we know spends the overwhelming majority of its existence in a unicellular phase — which does not always progress further. So aliens will most likely look like tiny, crawling creatures with no thoughts to interpret.

### Isn't Philosophy in University the Same as Logic?

No. Raise your hand if you skipped philosophy at university — the skipping rate speaks for itself.

- **Philosophy ≠ logic.** Logic here means the purely formal laws: syllogisms, their structure, and increasingly complex forms beyond simple "if A then B."
- Philosophy is a worthwhile subject, but far too remote for most people and certainly not for the school curriculum, since people absorb information poorly when it seems abstract — even when that abstraction directly affects their lives. Small children cannot be convinced philosophy is interesting; for older students it has more value.

**A biologist's bias, admitted openly:** the speaker would throw out a large share of current school subjects and keep **biology** at a level where people genuinely understand the difference between viruses and bacteria, why colds shouldn't be treated with antibiotics, and how vaccines work — basic, hard-wearing biology, repeated in every grade until it sticks like a prayer.

**A side observation:** because philosophy is only really studied by those who enjoy it, "super-creative" people from technical startups, space companies or IT firms sometimes announce grand insights in biology or philosophy that were first articulated two thousand years ago. It's genuinely nice they got there themselves — it shows a powerful mind — but it also reveals a **serious educational deficit**. And simply adding such subjects to the curriculum doesn't work: philosophy was mandatory for essentially everyone with higher education in Russian universities. Who remembers anything from those courses?

### "You Seem Biased Toward Harshness — the Harsher We Learn, the Better People Become"

That was never said. "A thought once uttered is a lie" — the exact opposite was argued: **everything is complicated.**

The urge to seek simple solutions is itself a dangerous distortion, and **populism grows out of it**. There are no simple answers to complex questions, but we always crave them, because we have laundry to do, children to put to bed, a report to prepare, and no appetite for complex answers on top of that. It simply doesn't work that way.
