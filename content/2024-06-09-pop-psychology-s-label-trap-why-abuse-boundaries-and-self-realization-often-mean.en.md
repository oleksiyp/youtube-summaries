# Pop Psychology: When Psychological Labels Replace Real Understanding

## The Phenomenon of "Popularized" Psychology

Psychology has moved decisively into mainstream culture, and the results are worth examining closely. Everyday emotional experiences are now routinely re-described in clinical-sounding language:

- Instead of "I feel hurt," people say **"I'm being devalued."**
- Any family quarrel or expression of dissatisfaction is instantly labeled **"abuse."**
- Everyone talks about **boundaries**, while almost no one talks about **dignity**.

The core problem is that these terms can be interpreted in almost any way. People feel they understand what they're saying, but this is an illusion of comprehension rather than genuine insight into processes that carry serious psychological consequences.

## Misused Term #1: "Abuse"

### What abuse actually is

True abuse is connected to **psychopathy** — a condition that essentially cannot be treated, for the simple reason that psychopaths do not go to psychologists. Psychopaths make up only about **8% of society**, so genuinely encountering abuse is far rarer than the constant use of the word suggests.

Real abuse has specific criteria:
- A particular structure of the abuser's personality
- Specific goals the abuser pursues
- A characteristic way the dynamic unfolds — victims of real abuse recognize this well

### What abuse is not

- Not every family scandal is abuse
- Not every complaint one person makes about another is abuse

Most conflicts are simply quarrels, misunderstandings, or difficulties in a relationship. These do **not** qualify as destructive relational patterns, and crucially, **they can be overcome**. Genuine abuse, by contrast, is practically insurmountable. Confusing the two closes off the possibility of repair.

## Misused Term #2: "Self-Realization"

Everyone speaks of self-realization, but few ask the essential question: **what exactly are you going to realize?**

Asking this question puts much into place. Examples of a real answer:

- "I know I'm meticulous and precise, I can sit patiently with detailed work for long stretches — so I'll work with numbers or learn to write software."
- "I can't sit still for five minutes; I get bored — so I need live work with people, where I'll feel good and achieve real results."

### The common misuse

Often "self-realization" is invoked by people who have no idea where to go, dislike everything, and are actually in a state of **apathy**. In such cases the correct sequence is:

1. Understand the causes of the apathy
2. Identify where the disappointment occurred
3. Draw out what the person is genuinely strong at, what they enjoy, what gives them a sense of drive
4. Only then discuss realizing a desire or dream — and learning to earn from what you love

## Misused Term #3: "Boundaries" vs. the Sense of Dignity

### The problem with boundary-talk

"Boundaries" have become an essentially **defensive posture** against other people. Enormous energy is spent on active defense: declaring what may and may not be done to you, announcing it loudly, listing the rules. Some people go so far as to hang their declarations on the refrigerator with magnets so a partner won't forget what's forbidden — a genuinely sad image of a "fortified" boundary.

The danger: people become so absorbed in defending the perimeter that they forget **what is inside**.

### The alternative: cultivating dignity

If a person instead works on growing and strengthening an internal **sense of dignity** — seeing the world differently, understanding what is theirs and what is not — then everything else follows naturally:

- Saying **"no"** becomes easy
- Carrying oneself in various situations so that no one would even think to pry with improper questions
- Being able to not react to provocations

Dignity is, first and foremost, **respect toward the other person**. It doesn't permit rudeness, doesn't permit intruding on someone else's territory, doesn't permit inappropriate questions. And others read it: they sense it would be awkward to ask this person such things.

A person with dignity may still defend what we call personal boundaries — in many different ways — but it will always be *about dignity*, not about erecting a jagged fence that no one may cross. Yet we hear about boundaries from every direction, and about how to cultivate dignity, almost nothing.

## The Real Danger: Labeling Instead of Living

### Labels applied to others

Rather than genuine inquiry, popular psychology often becomes a **label-sticking exercise**. Before calling something abuse, it's worth asking: is it really? Perhaps it's simply a large mismatch — "we're whipping up foam, but at the bottom there's clean water." Perhaps the situation can be sorted out and brought into a form that suits the couple. Behind convenient, easily pronounced terms there may be simple pain, while the person fights windmills.

Whenever such words come up, the right response is to demand decoding: **what actually stands behind this word for you?**

### Labels applied to oneself

Reading popular psychology books, one can discover absolutely every described condition in oneself — because:

- The descriptions are written in very general terms
- The symptomatology is described vaguely
- Determining whether a problem genuinely exists requires serious diagnostics

This mirrors medical textbooks: as medical students joke, by the third year everyone has found every disease in themselves — even the boys find pregnancy. Symptoms are not the root of what's happening. In pop psychology, people eagerly pin diagnosis after diagnosis on themselves until they conclude there's no way forward.

### Not everything needs fixing

- Not everything should be subject to psychological correction
- A person can face difficulties, cope with them, make their own decisions — right or wrong — and simply live through their life
- There is a real risk of **refusing to live one's own unique life**, with its particular features and situations and inner world, in an attempt to comb everything smooth via psychology until it matches the book
- This destroys the **aroma of individual life**
- Not everything in you or your partner requires correction; not every life situation requires a consultation or advice

Some challenges are better accepted alone — and by accepting them and emerging victorious, you actually grow and develop.

## When the Psychologist Becomes a Crutch

Having a personal psychologist or psychotherapist has become fashionable — running to them at the slightest cold draft. Two motives drive this:

1. **Social conformity** — "it's the done thing," comme il faut
2. **Delegating responsibility** — "let them sort it out; I'll listen to what they say and then decide"

This is excessive, and anything excessive demands attention and analysis.

## Closing Guidance

- Don't rush to apply labels
- Don't run immediately to even a trusted psychologist every time life gets rough — life exists precisely to give us challenges we can overcome
- This does **not** apply to people genuinely needing psychological help — those who cannot cope, who are sinking into severe suffering they can't escape alone. That is not pop psychology; those are serious matters.
- What is unhelpful is using the beacons scattered through popular books to diagnose yourself and those around you, and to juggle fashionable terminology
